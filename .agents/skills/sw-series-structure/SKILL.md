## 1. Fundamental Differences: Television vs. Feature Film

1. **Compressed, Muscular Scenes:**
   - In a feature film, a dramatic set piece can sprawl across 5–7 pages.
   - In television, the target length for a standard dramatic scene is **~2 pages**. A 4-page dialogue meeting is an immediate red flag signaling drag.
2. **Simulates the Edited Broadcast:**
   - A teleplay simulates the rhythm of an edited episode, giving the reader the visceral sensation of watching television in real time.
3. **Dramatic Scenes vs. Logistical Scenes:**
   - A change in physical location (`slugline`) is merely a **logistical scene**.
   - A **Dramatic Scene** is governed by a character's active objective: a single dramatic beat may span multiple connected sluglines. Measure and pace your outline by dramatic scenes.
4. **Format (Logistical) vs. Structure (Dramatic):**
   - Act breaks historically originated from external logistical realities (theatre candle changes, film reel swaps, television commercial breaks).
   - **Act counts are delivery formats; the three-part dramatic progression (Setup → Confrontation → Resolution) is the underlying engine.**
5. **Production Scheduling Discipline:**
   - Production departments track day/night schedules from sluglines. Stick strictly to `DAY` or `NIGHT`. Avoid writing `DAWN` or `DUSK` unless lighting is an absolute story requirement.
6. **Structure is Inherited:**
   - Every subsequent episode of a series inherits the act breakdown, pacing, and tone established by the Pilot script. Writing the pilot defines the structural template for the entire run.

## 2. The Episode Container: Acts, Pages, and Teasers

### 2.1 The Classic Four-Act Broadcast Functional Chain
- **Act One:** Establish characters, status quo, the episode catalyst, and primal stakes.
- **Act Two Break:** Must propel the narrative into a **startlingly new and unexpected direction** (e.g., in a legal procedural, arresting an unexpected suspect who flips the case).
- **Act Three Break (The Lowest Valley):** The protagonist's initial strategy implodes; the hero believes they have lost control: *"There is no hope."*
- **Act Four:** Formulating the ultimate desperate tactic, facing the crisis, and executing resolution.

### 2.2 Scene Count Discipline
- Average one-hour episode: **22 to 30 dramatic scenes** (~5 to 7 scenes per act).
- Standard 1-hour broadcast scripts target **50 to 60 pages** (representing 42–48 minutes of screen time without commercials).

### 2.3 Expanding from Four to Five or Six Acts
- **Five-Act Model (Standard Cable / Network):** Introduces a standalone Teaser, splitting Act Two or Three to create tighter mid-story cliffhangers.
- **Six-Act Model (Modern Broadcast):** Splits Acts Two and Three into four shorter quadrants, ensuring commercial breaks occur every 8–10 minutes. Act Six often resolves the immediate crisis in 3 pages, spending the final 3 pages launching the cliffhanger for the next episode.

### 2.4 Teasers & Cold Opens
- **Runtime:** Typically 2 to 5 pages (rarely exceeding 8–10 pages).
- **Function:** Grab the viewer before the main title sequence.
- **The Golden Rule for Beginners (Pamela Douglas):** Launch with **dramatic story action**, not abstract philosophical narration or thematic lectures.
- **Flashback Teaser Trap:** You may begin in medias res during an intense climax (e.g., *Breaking Bad*), **but you must never answer the central dramatic question in the teaser**. Cut away before the decisive outcome is revealed.
- **Cold Open vs. Teaser:** A pure cold open is often an isolated comedic or thematic vignette that does not directly ignite the A-story plotline.

### 2.5 Discovering Invisible Acts in Premium Streaming Scripts
Premium streaming scripts (HBO, Netflix) rarely display physical `ACT ONE` headings on the page, yet they still obey rigorous act structures. Measured across master scripts (*Succession*, *Mad Men*), look for **The Three Invisible Act Criteria**:
1. **Story Day / Shift in Time Block:** Crossing into a new day or distinct time block marks an act transition.
2. **Radical Location / Transit Shift:** Packing up, boarding a plane, or relocating to an entirely new arena.
3. **Power or Information Reversal:** The moment the core dramatic question pivots or authority decisively shifts between characters.
- *Streamers disguise acts by anchoring episodes within rigid operational containers (e.g., board meetings, shareholder summits, weddings, funerals).*

### 2.6 The Choice to Label Acts on the Page
- **Include `ACT ONE` Headings:** When writing spec episodes for broadcast networks, basic cable, or AVOD platforms that require commercial insertions.
- **Omit Act Headings:** When targeting premium cable (HBO) or subscription streaming (SVOD) where continuous narrative immersion is standard.

## 3. Act-Outs, Cliffhangers, and Information Management

### 3.1 Genuine Cliffhangers vs. Cheap "Schmuck Bait"
- **Schmuck Bait (The False Cliffhanger):** Manufacturing a cheap physical shock right before a commercial break, only to undo it immediately in the next second (e.g., a flatlining heart monitor that miraculously resumes beeping 2 seconds later).
- **The Modern Principle (Neil Landau):** *"Forcing a cliffhanger at the end of each act break is deadly... Act breaks must land on an authentic turning point that emerges from character choices and moral risk, not merely plot shock."*
- **The Test of Authentic Cliffhangers:** The twist must be subtly prepared so that when it hits, the audience feels: *"I should have seen that coming, but I didn't."*

### 3.2 Neil Landau’s Ten Cliffhanger Types
1. **Character in Peril:** Visceral physical or legal jeopardy.
2. **The Ticking Clock:** A deadline is suddenly shortened.
3. **Love in the Balance:** A relationship is irrevocably severed or confessed.
4. **Surprise Twist / Epiphany:** A revelation that recontextualizes all previous events.
5. **Death / Loss of a Key Figure:** Removing an emotional or institutional anchor.
6. **Discovery of a Secret:** An incriminating truth is unearthed by the worst possible person.
7. **Major Life Event:** Unexpected pregnancy, firing, sudden arrest.
8. **Lack of Closure:** Leaving a character stranded in emotional limbo.
9. **Mini-Cliffhanger:** Micro-suspense beats preceding scene cuts.
10. **Comedy Cliffhanger:** An absurd, irreversible social embarrassment.

### 3.3 Eight Forms of Act-Out Execution
1. A loaded dramatic question left hanging.
2. A single devastating reversal line of dialogue.
3. A shocking emotional confession.
4. An active, silent physical gesture (no dialogue).
5. A lingering close-up on a loaded prop.
6. Repeating a dialogue catchphrase with its meaning completely inverted.
7. An interrupted setup where the payoff is cut short.
8. An irreversible tactical commitment.
*Rule: Distribute act-outs across different storylines (A, B, or C). At least one act-out per episode should rely entirely on silent physical action or a prop, rather than dialogue.*

### 3.4 The Four Information Management Tools (Peter Oberg)
- Dramatic storytelling is governed by: **Who knows what, and when?**
1. **Dramatic Irony:** The audience knows a vital truth that the character does not know. Generates deep, agonizing suspense as we watch them walk blindly toward danger.
2. **Surprise:** Both character and audience learn a shocking truth at the exact same moment.
3. **Mystery:** The character and audience are both in the dark, actively working together to decode clues.
4. **Suspense:** The audience knows what is likely to happen, but is tortured by not knowing *when* or *how* it will hit.
- *Red Line: Never keep the protagonist knowing vital story information that is arbitrarily hidden from the audience for cheap mystery.*

## 4. Weaving Multi-Strand Narratives: A, B, C Plots and Runners

### 4.1 Defining the Strands
- **A-Story (The Franchise Spine):** The primary outer plot engine that drives the episode’s main stakes and commands the most screen time.
- **B-Story (The Relationship / Personal Core):** The emotional, romantic, or moral subplot that reveals character vulnerability.
- **C-Story / Runner:** A smaller comedic, domestic, or thematic thread (often 2–3 short beats) that injects tonal contrast and texture.

### 4.2 Hard Rules of Strand Weaving
1. **The A-Story Anchors the Episode:** Runs through every act; act breaks land predominantly on A-story turning points (or pivotal B-story crossroads).
2. **Never Close an Act on a C-Story:** If a subplot beat is strong enough to carry an act break, it is an A or B story, not a C-story.
3. **The Line of Interest:** Avoid mechanical, rigid alternating patterns (A → B → A → B). Follow the audience's emotional curiosity.
4. **Elliptical Compression:** Cutting away to the B-story creates the illusion of elapsed time, allowing A-story events to progress naturally without boring transitional filler.
5. **No Scene Exists Solely for Exposition:** If facts must be delivered, embed them directly within an active emotional argument or high-stakes tactical task.

### 4.3 Alternative Breaking Frameworks
- **Break by Character, Then Weave (Wendy West / Dexter):** Map out each lead character’s complete emotional arc (Beginning, Middle, Climax) on separate whiteboards, then weave them together chronologically.
- **The Core Question Method (Glen Mazzara / The Walking Dead):** Reject rigid A/B/C lettering. Ask: *"What is this episode about as a unified moral experience?"* Anchor every character to that shared thematic question.

## 5. Kam Miller’s Five-Part Dramatic Scene Structure

Every compelling television scene contains five structural components:
1. **Hero's Want:** A concrete objective locked before the scene begins.
2. **Obstacle:** Immediate resistance from another character or environment.
3. **Escalations (Minimum 3):** Progressive tactical moves and counter-moves. *Prioritize physical actions over mere verbal arguing to prevent the teleplay from sounding like a radio drama.*
4. **Decision:** The climax of the scene—the character is forced to make a choice under pressure.
5. **Resolution:** The immediate fallout of that choice, propelling the character into the next scene.
- *Rule: An emotional reaction without an active want is an image, not a dramatic scene.*

## 6. Season Architecture: Shapes, Arcs, and Episodes

1. **Tentpoles:** Major tentpole episodes (Premiere, Mid-Season Climax, Penultimate Reversal, Season Finale) anchor the macro-narrative.
2. **Bottle Episodes:** Born from budget constraints, confining the core cast to a single existing set with minimal guest actors. Use bottle episodes to force long-repressed interpersonal secrets to detonate.
3. **Container / Ceremony Episodes:** Structuring an episode around a formal, ticking-clock real-world ceremony (a high-stakes wedding, a corporate summit, an election night, a funeral). The ceremony's predetermined schedule provides a built-in narrative clock.
4. **The Six-Episode Season Shape (Fleabag Template):**
   - *Ep 1 (The Pitch / World):* Establish voice, desires, and current coping mechanisms.
   - *Ep 2 (The Pursuit):* Active quest toward a new, enticing objective.
   - *Ep 3 (The Fracture):* Deep-seated vulnerability exposed.
   - *Ep 4 (The Pressure Chamber / Bottle):* Trapped together; defensive masks shatter.
   - *Ep 5 (False Triumph):* Apparent victory achieved, setting up the trap.
   - *Ep 6 (The Reckoning):* Final confrontation, moral accounting, thematic resolution.
5. **Season Finales:** Must resolve the central dramatic question of *this specific season*, while simultaneously planting the seeds of unresolved interpersonal conflict for the next season.

## 7. Episode Diagnostic Checklist

1. Can you state the core dramatic question of this episode in one concise sentence?
2. Does every character strand (A, B, C) possess a clear objective attached to a lead character?
3. Does the A-story run through every act, with major act breaks landing on A or B reversals?
4. Are act breaks free of lazy "schmuck bait"? Do they emerge from character risk and moral dilemmas?
5. Does every scene contain the Five-Part Structure (Want, Obstacle, at least 3 Escalations, Decision, Resolution)?
6. Does any scene drag beyond 3 pages without an internal tactical shift?
7. Is there at least one act-out executed through silent physical action or a prop, rather than dialogue?
8. Are scenes free of dry, unmotivated exposition?
9. In streaming scripts without act headings, can the acts be clearly identified using story-day shifts or power reversals?
10. Does the final act resolve the episode's crisis while leaving audience anticipation primed for the next installment?

## 8. Step-by-Step Workflow: Breaking an Episode

1. **Lock the Format & Engine:** Define series format, runtime, and the central operational question of the episode.
2. **Identify Comp Structures:** Select a premier existing series with matching tone to benchmark scene counts and act rhythms.
3. **Draft the Strand Loglines:** Write one active logline for each plotline (A, B, C).
4. **Anchor Key Milestones:** Lock the Teaser, the Midpoint Reversal, the All-Is-Lost valley, and the Climax.
5. **Map the Scene Grid:** Lay out 22–28 scenes across the acts, labeling character want and immediate conflict.
6. **Assign Act-Outs:** Verify that each act break lands on a distinct, earned turning point.
7. **Draft the Scene Beat Sheet:** Outline each scene's five-part progression (Want, Obstacle, Escalations, Decision, Resolution).
8. **Draft Pages:** Write with velocity, targeting ~2 pages per dramatic scene.
9. **Run the Diagnostic Audit:** Tighten scene entrances, eliminate exposition, and polish transitions.
