# Korean & French Screenwriting Methodologies

> **Output Language = Prompt Language**; terminology is strictly anchored to industry-standard screenwriting concepts (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)). Never invent non-standard translations.

The distinct development ecosystems, narrative mechanics, and survival strategies of South Korean and French screenwriters. Derived from the *Storytelling Masterclasses (International Edition)* featuring three master writer-directors:
- **Oh Seung-uk** (South Korea: *Christmas in August, Green Fish, The Shameless, Kill Boksoon*).
- **Choi Seok-hwan** (South Korea: *King and the Clown, Radio Star, The Happy Life, Lost in Hong Kong*).
- **Frédéric Auburtin** (France: *Paris, je t'aime; The Secret Agents; The Bridge; United Passions*).

*(For Japanese methodologies, see `sw-japanese-screenwriting` and `ozu-screenplay-style`. For American studio development, see `sw-american-case-studies` and `sw-industry-business`.)*

### Shared Foundations
Despite radical cultural differences, all three masters share three non-negotiable operational principles:
1. **Field Research Precedes Typing**: Immerse yourself in the physical soil, architecture, and hidden subcultures before outlining.
2. **Read the Script Aloud to a Hostile Audience**: Test your pages before peers who harbor creative grudges or professional skepticism.
3. **Survive the Producers**: *"Persuade them when you can; when you cannot, swallow your frustration, find a brilliant workaround, and protect the soul of the work."*

## I. Oh Seung-uk: Screenwriting as an Endurance Profession

### 1. Rendering Authentic Emotion on the Page
- *Christmas in August* was initially dismissed by producers as commercially unviable. Oh Seung-uk rewrote action lines with the sensory texture of short prose (washing hands: the red plastic basin, the sound of splashing water, black rain droplets soaking into a clean white shirt), adhering to Lee Chang-dong's ethos: *"What 1990s Korean cinema lacks most is authentic emotional atmosphere."*
- **The Anti-Melodrama Rule for Terminal Illness**:
  - The protagonist is informed of his impending death in the opening minutes rather than saving the diagnosis for a third-act tear-jerker.
  - *"Since the real world is already overwhelmed by tragedy, exploiting human mortality purely for cheap commercial profit is artistically dishonest."*
  - Authentic dying people clean their fountain pens, ride bicycles, laugh with their sisters over watermelon, and draft VCR instruction manuals for their elderly fathers so life continues smoothly after they are gone.

### 2. Undercover Field Research
- **Physical Immersion**: Director Park Kwang-su traveled to remote islands to study soil color, tree bark textures, and local property disputes before writing. Lee Chang-dong walked everywhere on foot: *"A writer who drives everywhere loses their connection to the earth."*
- **The Undercover Protocol**:
  - To write homicide detectives, send a young assistant to live inside a violent crimes precinct for a week, logging real arrest records.
  - To write organized crime, Oh Seung-uk worked as an errand boy for local gang bosses, discovering that real mobsters argue childishly over wedding guest money like petulant teenagers.
  - To write hostess bars, interview the male clients rather than the hostesses—clients reveal their true vanities, providing an unexpected sociological perspective.
  - When subjects resist a screenwriter, disguise your identity as a low-level production apprentice.
- **The Truth-Telling Liar**: Lee Chang-dong describes personal experiences from a detached, objective third-person perspective. The screenwriter is a master counterfeiter: **delivering profound emotional truth through carefully constructed narrative lies**.

### 3. Structural Design & Physical Space
- **Designing Act I and Act III First**: Lock down the opening image and the final climax before charting the middle. In *The Shameless*, Oh began with a single paradoxical image: *"From a distance, it appears to be a lovers' embrace; up close, the woman is driving a knife into the detective's gut."*
- **Drafting 60 Scenes, Leaving 60 Blank**: In a 120-scene feature, outline 60 core structural pillars, then leave the remaining 60 scenes blank. Drive through deteriorating urban neighborhoods with the production designer, allowing real buildings and alleyways to trigger spontaneous staging.
- **Physical Spaces as the Structural Skeleton**: In *The Shameless*, the heroine moves three times, each apartment physically mapping her moral descent:
  $$\text{Upscale Gangnam High-Rise} \longrightarrow \text{Transitional Hideout} \longrightarrow \text{Decaying Slum Near an Abattoir}$$
  *The ceiling gets lower, the walls get moldier, and twenty pairs of muddy boots cluster by the communal door.*
- **The Transition-Point Slump**: In a 120-minute feature, audiences check their watches around Minute 70 (Scene 70). Mediocre directors force arbitrary explosions; master screenwriters use this lull to ignite an intense, intimate personal confrontation between the leads.

### 4. Directing Antagonism
- Write villains with more care than the protagonist. Infuse the antagonist with the most malicious things you have done in your own life, alongside the cruelest things others have done to you.
- Subvert the macho Korean hard-boiled trope: give the tough male lead a tired, slumped posture and quiet feminine domestic traits.

### 5. Surviving Producers & Notes
- **Read Aloud to Your Enemies**: Gather 5–6 cynical peers and read the entire screenplay aloud from morning to night. When a scene is mocked, do not argue—figure out what setup is missing.
- **Save Your Cherished Scenes by Making Them Structural Load-Bearers**: If producers want to cut a poetic, non-essential scene you love, move it into the most urgent crisis of the second act (e.g., a priest dancing with a child during an enemy siege). Make the scene indispensable to the resolution.
- **"Remove My Name from the Screen"**: The screenwriter's ultimate nuclear option when studio rewrites gut the soul of the narrative.

## II. Choi Seok-hwan: Commercial Genre Craft & The Triad

### 1. The Korean Production Triad
The engine of South Korean cinema's dominance:
```
[The Producer] ↔ [The Director] ↔ [The Screenwriter]
```
- **The Producer**: Represents the audience's investment and emotional satisfaction.
- **The Director**: The narrative vampire, hungry for unforgettable imagery.
- **The Screenwriter**: Bridges both worlds, serving the story with supreme craft.
- **The Living-Together Model**: The triad rents a room, eats together, and brainstorms 16 hours a day until the draft is cracked.

### 2. Genre as a Sacred Promise to the Audience
- Narrative structure is for the writer; **genre is a contract signed with the audience at the box office**.
- **The Rule of Unwavering Tone**:
  - In a comedy, the laughter must never stop, even if the protagonist dies tragically in the final frames.
  - In a detective thriller, no amount of humor may obscure the procedural investigation.
  - Innovation is limited to **5–10%** beyond established genre tropes. If you blend genres, each genre element must score 100% on its own terms.

### 3. The Parable of the Grandmother's Tale (Qualitative Escalation)
In the traditional folklore of the Tiger and the Mother:
1. *Setup*: The mother walks through the mountain pass carrying rice cakes.
2. *First Encounter*: The tiger demands a rice cake.
3. *Qualitative Escalation*: The tiger does not merely ask for a second rice cake; **he eats the mother's arm, then devours the mother entirely and disguises himself in her clothing**. Escalations must represent a **qualitative change in nature**, not a mere quantitative accumulation of threats.
4. *The Double Reversal Climax*:
   - *Reversal 1*: The children trapped in the tree pray, and heaven lowers a sturdy rope to save them.
   - *Reversal 2*: The tiger prays, but heaven lowers a rotten straw rope; the tiger falls to his death.
- *The Rule*: **A single reversal at the climax is never enough. Always engineer two distinct twists to deliver a bittersweet catharsis.**

### 4. The Dual Helix: Deepening Crisis & Intensifying Desire
$$\textbf{External Danger Escalates} \uparrow \quad \times \quad \textbf{Internal Obsession / Desire Intensifies} \uparrow$$
- A compelling protagonist must be driven by an undeniable desire while being stalked by a specific, persistent terror.
- **The Sacrifice Equation**: It is not interesting to watch a billionaire risk \$1 million to make \$10 million. It is electrifying to watch a billionaire risk his entire \$10 billion fortune to rescue his lost Persian cat. **A character's agency is measured by what they are willing to lose.**
- **The Overnight Transformation**: Even if the entire film takes place over a single evening, the protagonist must emerge radically, irreversibly transformed.

## III. Frédéric Auburtin: The Director-Screenwriter Synthesis

### 1. The French Auteur Ecosystem
- Supported by the CNC (National Center of Cinema) levy system: box office revenues from Hollywood blockbusters are taxed to subsidize original French screenwriting.
- The director holds absolute legal right to final cut, making the screenwriter-director collaboration an intimate artistic marriage.

### 2. Dialogue Written Absolute Last
Jean-Paul Rappeneau's (*Cyrano de Bergerac*) iron discipline:
$$\text{Draft Narrative Treatment} \longrightarrow \text{Stress-Test Scene Architecture} \longrightarrow \textbf{Write Dialogue Last}$$
Novice screenwriters begin typing `INT. APARTMENT - NIGHT` and immediately launch into casual banter. Master screenwriters design the visual geometry and subtext first; dialogue is merely the final brushstroke.

### 3. Clint Eastwood's Dictum: "12 Lines of Dialogue, Not 13"
- **Silence is More Powerful than Talk**: A pause carries more emotional velocity than a musical note.
- **The Boxing Ring of Subtext**: Dialogue must operate like a fencing match where characters mask their true intentions:
  - *"I'm sorry I'm late"* = *"I despise being here, so I made you wait."*
  - *"You look remarkably well"* = *"I am civilized enough not to insult you to your face."*
- **Play It, Don't Say It**: If an emotion, power dynamic, or plot development can be expressed through a glance, an edit, or an action, **delete the line immediately**.

### 4. The Italian-Style Table Read
1. Map every scene onto a massive physical wall chart.
2. Gather the principal cast for 2–3 full days of reading.
3. **The Actors Read Everything Monotonously**: Character names, sluglines, parentheticals, and action lines are read aloud in an uninflected cadence without "acting."
4. Record the entire reading. Listening to the audio reveals rhythm, bloated scenes, and structural dead zones before shooting a single frame.

### 5. Assembling *Paris, je t'aime*: Emotional Logic Over Narrative Continuity
When coordinating 20 world-renowned directors across 20 Parisian arrondissements, narrative logic broke down into an incoherent 4-hour disaster. Auburtin resolved the film in post-production:
- **Discard Plot Logic; Honor Emotional Rhythms**: Re-edited the film across **82 distinct cuts**, sequencing segments purely by emotional juxtaposition (curiosity/wit $\to$ grief/solitude $\to$ transcendence).
- **The Kuleshov Cut**: Placing the comedic mime sequence between a grieving mother and a lonely tourist created an emergent third meaning: the shared collective heartbeat of Paris.

## IV. Master Actionable Rules

1. **Investigate Before Typing**: Travel to the physical locations; inspect soil, lighting, and dialect; conduct undercover interviews; write with your feet.
2. **Paint the Emotion, Not the Description**: Ground character feelings in tactile physical details (the color of a plastic basin, black rain droplets on a white shirt); avoid excessive adjectives that blur dramatic focus.
3. **Anchor the Bookends First**: Design Act I and the final scene of Act III before wandering into the second act.
4. **Use Physical Space as a Structural Skeleton**: Track character arcs through deteriorating or shifting domestic environments (e.g., three degrading apartments).
5. **Overcome the Minute-70 Slump**: Anticipate audience fatigue at the 60–70 minute mark by igniting a deep character confrontation rather than inserting cheap spectacle.
6. **Read Aloud to Hostile Peers**: Test your draft by having critics read it aloud; use monotonous Italian-style table reads to identify bloated dialogue.
7. **Protect Cherished Scenes Through Structural Necessity**: If an emotional scene risks being cut by executives, weave it directly into the mechanics of the main crisis.
8. **Honor the Genre Contract**: Deliver on the genre's baseline promise (comedies must produce constant laughter; mysteries must deliver rigorous clues); limit innovation to 5–10% variations.
9. **The Double Reversal Climax**: A single twist at the climax is predictable; engineer two consecutive reversals to deliver a bittersweet catharsis.
10. **The Dual Helix of Tension**: Escalate external danger while simultaneously intensifying the protagonist's obsessive desire.
11. **Measure Agency by Sacrifice**: Characters reveal their true values only when they risk losing everything they hold dear.
12. **Write Dialogue Absolute Last**: Lock down visual blocking and dramatic objectives before penning spoken words.
13. **Obey the 12-Line Cap**: Prune dialogue ruthlessly; allow pregnant silences and visual actions to carry the narrative weight.
14. **Subvert Institutional Meddling with Visual Wit**: Protect controversial themes from censors by embedding them in subtle visual symbols.
15. **Edit by Emotional Geometry**: When linear narrative logic fails, assemble scenes based on emotional contrast and tonal momentum.
16. **Preserve Character Dignity**: Never resolve an antagonist or flawed character through gratuitous humiliation; maintain narrative grace.
