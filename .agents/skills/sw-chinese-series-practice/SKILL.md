# Chinese Series Practice & Production Ecosystem

> **Output Language = Prompt Language**; terminology is strictly anchored to industry-standard screenwriting concepts (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)). Never invent non-standard translations.

The professional craft, document pipeline, adaptation mechanics, and regulatory/production realities of Mainland Chinese television drama (*Guochanju*). Synthesized from five foundational industry texts:
- **Yao Kougen, *Introduction to TV Series Writing* (Shanghai Theatre Academy Series, 2016)**: The academic canon—tripartite nature of television, genre taxonomies, four Chinese teleplay formats, episode-level structural mechanics, four tiers of suspense, and 36 dramatic situations.
- **Zhang Wei et al., *TV Series Adaptation Course* (China Film Press, 2014)**: Adaptation engineering—five source archetypes, selling-point migration, thematic coordinate systems, segment-by-segment copy/delete/adapt protocols, and six full case studies.
- **Zhang Mingzhi & Song Peiyi, *TV Producer Course* (China Radio & TV Press, 2016)**: Production and legal realities—teleplay review states, the "Dramatic Eye" (戏眼), strict time limits, regulatory reviews, scene-breakdown sheets, and contracts.
- **Zhao Binbin, *Film & TV Fragment Adaptation* (2016)**: Fragment compression craft (compressing features into 8–20 minute stage scenes).
- **Translated US Series Terminology (Pamela Douglas & Richard Blum Chinese Editions)**: Standardized mapping between Hollywood terms and Chinese studio vernacular.

## I. Chinese Television vs. US Television: Comparative Matrix

| Dimension | Mainland Chinese Drama (*Guochanju*) | US Television Series | Operational Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structural Unit** | **Episode / Segment / Scene (集 / 段 / 场)**. *"Theatre requires acts and scenes; film requires shots and sequences; television requires scenes, segments, and episodes"* (Yao). | Acts + Commercial Act-Outs | Use Episode/Segment/Scene for Chinese platforms; translate to Act grids for Western co-productions. |
| **Episode Endings** | **Li Yu's "Shousha" (收煞)**: Restraining the cymbals to leave the audience guessing what happens next + Song dynasty storytelling cliffhangers (*"Listen to the next chapter"*). | Act-outs engineered around commercial broadcast breaks. | Use Chinese narrative cliffhangers for emotional hooks; use page anchors for pacing. |
| **Repetition** | **Repetition is an essential aesthetic**: Pattern repetition, plot motifs, recurring props, and bookend motifs are celebrated across 30+ episodes. | Repetition = wasted screen time. | Employ deliberate repetition for 30+ episode daily broadcast dramas; eliminate for 12-episode streaming mini-series. |
| **Protagonist Agency** | **Passive protagonists are permitted**: *"Enduring suffering while harboring a persistent, suppressed desire"* (e.g., *Oshin*, family ethics dramas). | Passive protagonists = fatal structural defect. | Permitted in female resilience epics and domestic sagas, provided she takes **at least one active choice per episode**. |
| **Season Architecture** | **Closed, Completed Narrative**: Full series is drafted, shot, and aired in its entirety, requiring strict structural closure (Zhang Wei). | Open series engine designed for indefinite renewal. | Satellite broadcast dramas must be fully closed; streaming originals may plant sequel hooks. |
| **Bottle Episodes** | **Prohibited**: A single-location episode with two supporting characters is viewed by producers as unbroadcastable and amateurish (Zhang Wei). | Celebrated artistic and budgetary device. | Chinese productions compress budgets by **batch-shooting shared standing sets across multiple episodes** instead of isolating single bottle episodes. |
| **Lead Absences** | **Prohibited**: The lead protagonist must appear prominently in every single episode. | Ensemble rotation episodes permitted. | The lead must drive the central conflict of every episode. |
| **Dialogue Density** | **Intentional redundancy**: Domestic viewers multitask; dialogue must carry 50%+ of screen time so viewers who look away do not lose the plot. | Extreme economy; action over talk. | Dialogue-heavy for broadcast; visual and cinematic for streaming thrillers. |
| **Theme Stability** | **"The ultimate censor is the audience, not the state"**: Themes must feature clear moral consequences, reward/punishment, and moral clarity. | Themes can remain ambiguous, cynical, or morally gray. | Mainstream broadcast requires clear moral arcs. |
| **In Medias Res** | **Forbids historical origin exposition**: Begin immediately with rain falling and wind blowing—events already in progress; weave exposition in sideways. | Pilots spend time establishing rules and backstory. | Drop viewers straight into ongoing physical crises. |
| **Unresolved Threads** | **Permitted in web structures**: Secondary character threads may fade out naturally without formal resolution (Yao). | Every planted setup must have a payoff. | Primary plots must resolve; secondary atmospheric threads may taper off. |
| **Episode Finale** | **Every episode must end on an unresolved suspense hook**. *(Streaming exception: replace cliffhangers with a clear debt settled in future episodes).* | The final act resolves the episodic case. | Chinese episodes must conclude on forward-leaning tension. |

### Word-to-Minute Conversion for Chinese Teleplays
Because Chinese scripts are measured in character counts rather than Western Courier-12 pages:
- **Dialogue-Heavy Drama (Broadcast / Melodrama)**: ~370–400 Chinese characters per minute (approx. **15,000–18,000 characters per 45-minute episode**).
- **Action / Cinematic Thriller (Streaming / Comedy)**: ~300–330 Chinese characters per minute (approx. **12,000–14,000 characters per episode**).
- *Half-Hour Comedy (28 mins net)*: ~**9,200 characters per episode**.

## II. The "One Case Across N Episodes" Spectrum

Chinese drama structure is defined by how many episodes a single conflict or case spans:

| Cadence | Structure Type | Strengths | Trade-Offs | Best Suited For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 Episode per Case** | Procedural / Sitcom | Easy casual viewing; high syndication value. | No cumulative character growth; feels repetitive by Episode 12. | Sitcoms, daily procedural dramas. |
| **2–3 Episodes per Case** | Serialized Procedural (*Mainstream Standard*) | Provides satisfying mini-resolutions while advancing serial character arcs. | Requires 8–10 distinct major cases per 24 episodes. | Legal, detective, workplace dramas. |
| **4–6 Episodes per Arc** | Block Movement (*Major Life Chapter*) | Deep emotional immersion and epic scope. | Slower early pacing; risks losing viewers in the setup. | Historical epics, family sagas. |
| **Full Season Single Arc** | Pure Serialized Thriller | Maximum tension and binge-watching momentum. | Weak syndication value; cannot be viewed out of order. | 12-episode streaming mini-series. |

## III. Volume and Running Time Constraints

- **Episode Counts**: Traditional broadcast dramas range from **30 to 50 episodes** (aired as two back-to-back episodes daily). Streaming originals favor **12, 16, or 24 episodes**.
- **Net Running Time (Broadcast Standard)**:
  - Total broadcast slot: **45 minutes**.
  - Net narrative running time: **Not less than 41 minutes 30 seconds**.
  - Opening title sequence: **60–90 seconds**.
  - End credits: **120–150 seconds**.
  - Always pace scripts for **41.5 net minutes**, leaving space for "Next Episode" previews.
- **Total Volume**: A standard 30-episode teleplay requires roughly **400,000 to 500,000 Chinese characters**, with dialogue comprising 50%+ of the total text.

## IV. The Document Delivery Chain

Three distinct development pipelines operate across the Chinese industry:

```
[Academic / Planning Pipeline (Yao)]
Concept Outline ➔ Character Portfolios ➔ Main Locations ➔ Creative Statement ➔ Sample Teleplay (Ep 1–2)

[Producer Review Pipeline (Zhang Mingzhi)]
Story Outline ➔ Episode-by-Episode Synopses (With "Dramatic Eye") ➔ Episodic Scripts ➔ Scene Breakdown

[Contractual Delivery Pipeline]
Outline & Pitch ➔ Master Bible & Bios ➔ Episode Synopses ➔ Batch 1 Drafts ➔ Revisions ➔ Shooting Lock
```

### The Concept of the "Dramatic Eye" (戏眼 - Xì Yǎn)
In Chinese producer reviews, every episodic synopsis must feature a clearly identifiable **Dramatic Eye**: a single, unforgettable dramatic confrontation, secret revelation, or moral choice that defines that specific episode. *If an episode cannot be summarized around its Dramatic Eye, it is structurally deficient.*

## V. Chinese Teleplay Formatting (*Chang Hao Zhi* - 场号制)

Mainland teleplays utilize a scene-number layout optimized for production scheduling:
- **Scene Heading**: Numbered sequentially (`第X场` or `场X`), followed by `Location`, `Interior/Exterior` (`内/外`), and `Time/Lighting` (`日/夜`).
- **Action Paragraphs**: Prefixed with a triangle marker (`△`) indicating visual/physical action.
- **Dialogue**: Centered or left-aligned as `CHARACTER NAME: Dialogue`.
- **Camera Directives Forbidden**: Terms like "Pan", "Tilt", or "Close-up" are omitted. Visual direction is implied through concrete physical actions.
- **Revision Numbering**: Added scenes during production use `19A`, `19B`; deleted scenes are marked as `[Scene 66 Deleted]`.

## VI. Episode-Level Structural Laws

### 1. The 3-Minute Hook + 15-Minute Segment Rhythm (Yao Kougen)
- **The First 3 Minutes**: Must hook the multitasking audience immediately with a disruption already in progress.
- **The 15-Minute Segment (片断戏剧结构)**: Originally born from commercial ad breaks, the 15-minute mark is retained in modern streaming as a psychological necessity: **every 15 minutes requires an internal turning point, a localized climax, or a transfer of power/information**.
- **The Episode-End Cliffhanger**: End on an unresolved dilemma. In streaming, where viewers binge episodes continuously, ensure the cliffhanger represents a **concrete debt settled in future episodes**.

### 2. The Four Tiers of Suspense
1. **Series Master Suspense**: The overarching question driving the franchise (e.g., *Can the family business survive? Will the real identity be exposed?*).
2. **Episode-End Suspense**: The cliffhanger that propels the viewer into the next hour.
3. **15-Minute Segment Suspense**: Localized mini-dilemmas that prevent scene drag.
4. **Opening Hook Suspense**: The immediate physical crisis that seizes attention.

> **The Single-Question Discipline**: Avoid the fatal trap of launching 9 convoluted concurrent mysteries (the *Huangchenggen'er* syndrome). Audiences disengage when overwhelmed by disparate riddles. Maintain **one clear central dramatic question**; only when that question is answered may the next major dilemma emerge.

### 3. Five Classical Delay Techniques (延宕五技巧)
When tension threatens to resolve too quickly, deploy:
1. *Gathering Storm Clouds*: Escalating the external stakes so solving the problem becomes twice as dangerous.
2. *Abrupt Interruption*: Halting the confrontation just as the truth is about to be revealed, pivoting to another storyline.
3. *Micro-Action Division*: Breaking a major action into several minor obstacle beats (e.g., before reaching the duel, the hero encounters flat tires, hostile guards, and broken keys).
4. *Expanding Social Breadth*: Involving unexpected third parties (parents, employers, authorities) who complicate the resolution.
5. *Rhythmic Contrast*: Modulating speed (fast physical action juxtaposed against quiet emotional stasis).

### 4. The First Three Episodes Rule (张巍)
In Chinese broadcasting and streaming acquisitions, **evaluators make greenlight decisions based almost exclusively on the first three episodes**.
- The core conflict, the world rules, the central suspense, and the lead protagonist must be established within Episodes 1–3.
- Core characters who enter late in the series must make a cameo or be actively discussed in Episode 1.

### 5. The Situational Engine (情境两步法)
1. **Step 1**: Establish a persistent dramatic situation (Vengeance, Assimilation, Misunderstanding, Rivalry).
2. **Step 2**: Force characters to struggle inside that situation repeatedly.
- *The Compounding Law*: Assign the protagonist **4 to 5 simultaneous situational pressures** (e.g., family feud + business debt + rebellious child + workplace rival), and mandate that **at least one situation mutates into a new form midway through the series** (revenge transforms into romance; rivalry transforms into alliance).

## VII. Adapting IP into Long-Form Drama (Expansion Craft)

When adapting a 200,000-word web novel into a 40-episode teleplay (requiring 500,000 words), screenwriters must **expand rather than pad**:
- **Selling-Point Migration**: Identify which novel elements appeal only to niche readers (e.g., internal monologues, stat tables) and replace them with broadcast-friendly emotional conflicts (family tensions, romantic triangles).
- **The Four Expansion Methods**:
  1. *Fleshing out Off-Screen Events*: Staging events that the novel merely summarized in a sentence.
  2. *Elevating Supporting Characters*: Transforming minor background figures into active thematic foils with their own parallel subplots.
  3. *Building Antagonistic Alliances*: Unifying disparate episodic villains into a cohesive antagonistic cabal.
  4. *Multiplying Obstacle Layers*: Adding institutional, legal, and familial hurdles to every romantic or professional objective.
- **The "Copy / Delete / Adapt" Protocol**: Systematically tag every chapter of the source material as `[COPY: Stage directly]`, `[DELETE: Omit entirely]`, or `[ADAPT: Restructure and expand]`.

## VIII. Production Chain, Regulatory Red Lines & Contracts

### 1. Regulatory Compliance (Censorship Guidelines)
Scripts submitted for official filing (*Beian* - 备案) and distribution licensing must respect core industry red lines:
- **Clarity of Moral Stance**: Antagonists must not be glorified as romantic anti-heroes; criminal methods must not be depicted as instructional how-to guides.
- **Institutional Integrity**: Depictions of state authorities (police, judiciary, military) require formal review by specialized consulting bureaus.
- **Historical Accuracy**: Established historical facts and revolutionary history cannot be rewritten into frivolous parodies.
- **Supernatural & Reincarnation**: Ghost stories, witchcraft, and time-travel reincarnation must be grounded in psychological delusions, theatrical dreams, or scientific phenomena.

### 2. Contractual Safeguards for Screenwriters
- **Step-Gated Remuneration**: Payments must follow discrete delivery stages (Concept $\to$ Bible $\to$ Synopses $\to$ Draft 1 $\to$ Revision $\to$ Production Lock).
- **Dual Sign-Off**: Secure written approvals from both the Producer and the Director before moving from episode outlines into full teleplays.
- **Credit Arbitration & Re-Shoots**: Contracts must define separation of rights and ensure the original screenwriter retains credit if rewrites do not exceed 50%.

## IX. Master Diagnostic Checklist

### Volume & Pacing
- [ ] Is every episode budgeted for 41.5 net minutes (~13,000–16,000 Chinese characters)?
- [ ] Does each episode contain 25–35 distinct scenes, anchored by 4–5 major dramatic confrontations?
- [ ] Does the opening 3 minutes feature an immediate disruption in progress?
- [ ] Is there an internal localized climax or power shift every 15 minutes?
- [ ] Does the episode conclude on an unresolved dramatic hook or a clearly logged narrative debt?

### Narrative Engine & Character
- [ ] Does the lead protagonist appear and drive the narrative in every single episode?
- [ ] Are the core premise, main conflict, and primary antagonist established within Episodes 1–3?
- [ ] Does every single episode possess a clearly identifiable "Dramatic Eye" (戏眼)?
- [ ] Is the protagonist navigating 4–5 concurrent situational pressures, with at least one mutating midway?
- [ ] Do the professional/career plot and the domestic/romantic subplot intertwine causally?

### Format & Compliance
- [ ] Is the teleplay formatted in standard scene-number layout (`第X场`, `△`, `CHARACTER: Dialogue`)?
- [ ] Are camera movements and shot sizes omitted from the literary script?
- [ ] Does the draft comply with regulatory guidelines regarding moral consequences and institutional integrity?
- [ ] Have specialized consulting experts been scheduled for medical, legal, or military storylines?

## X. Step-by-Step Chinese Drama Development Workflow

```
[1. Project Filing / Beian] ➔ [2. Master Proposal & Concept (策划书)]
       ➔ [3. Thematic Coordinates & Bible] ➔ [4. Episode-by-Episode Synopses (With Dramatic Eyes)]
       ➔ [5. Sample Teleplays (Ep 1–3)] ➔ [6. Producer/Director Sign-Off]
       ➔ [7. Batch Script Deliveries] ➔ [8. Scene Breakdown Sheet] ➔ [9. Regulatory Content Review]
```

1. **Step 1: Concept & Market Positioning**: Define the primary genre, target episode count (24/30/40), and commercial audience profile.
2. **Step 2: The Proposal Document (策划书)**: Draft the 1-page premise hook, the three protagonist innovations, the primary standing sets, and the thematic thesis.
3. **Step 3: Character Dossiers (人物小传)**: Define each character's flaw, secret motivation, and relationship triad.
4. **Step 4: Episode Synopses**: Construct the episode-by-episode grid. Ensure every episode features its specific *Dramatic Eye* and advances the 15-minute segment beats.
5. **Step 5: The Pilot Teleplays (Episodes 1–3)**: Draft the first three episodes with maximum velocity and scene density.
6. **Step 6: Dual Approval**: Secure formal written sign-offs on the outlines from both producer and director.
7. **Step 7: Batch Drafting**: Deliver scripts in 5–10 episode batches, adhering to the contractual step deal.
8. **Step 8: Scene-Breakdown Conversion (分场景表)**: Assist the script supervisor in categorizing scenes by location, cast, day/night, and crowd requirements for batch shooting.
9. **Step 9: Regulatory Review**: Revise drafts in coordination with legal and regulatory notes to secure the distribution license.
