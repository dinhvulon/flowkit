# Sitcom & Half-Hour Television Comedy Craft

> **Output Language = Prompt Language**; terminology is strictly anchored to industry-standard screenwriting concepts (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)). Never invent non-standard translations.

The core axiom of half-hour comedy: **Humor is not pasted on in post-production through clever dialogue; it grows organically from the narrative premise.** A comedy writer builds tension across three distinct levels:
$$\text{Premise} \longrightarrow \text{Sequence / Scene} \longrightarrow \text{Dialogue / Action}$$
Comedy seeded into the foundational premise *"automatically produces more, bigger, and more seamless laughs"* at the scene and dialogue levels (Evan S. Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms*, p. 42). The first task of a comedy writer is never inventing jokes; it is designing a **joke-producing machine**.

Writing a half-hour comedy is not simply shrinking a one-hour drama. It requires an **entire structural and stylistic system shift**: page count, typography, scene volume, joke density, structural units, and character psychology operate under entirely different laws. General scene craft, dialogue subtext, and television series engines are covered in `sw-scene-craft`, `sw-dialogue`, `sw-series-structure`, and `sw-series-engine-bible`.

Detailed layout specs, joke taxonomy examples, and structural templates are located in [reference.md](reference.md).

## I. Half-Hour Comedy vs. One-Hour Drama: The System Shift

| Dimension | Half-Hour Comedy | One-Hour Drama | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Page Count** | Single-Cam Film: **27–30**; Multi-Cam Tape: **45–50**; Animation: **40–50** | 48–63 | Smith, App. A; Landau |
| **Act Structure** | Cold Open + 2 Acts (Multi-cam) or 2–4 Acts (Single-cam) + Tag | Teaser + 4/5/6 Acts | Landau Tip 21 |
| **Act Length** | Teaser: 30–60s; Acts: 10–12 mins; Tag: $\le$ 60s | Acts: 10–15 mins | Blum p. 135 |
| **Act I Length** | **Extremely Brief (1–5 pages in a 30p script)** | ~25% of script | Smith p. 121 |
| **Scene Count** | Multi-Cam: **15–20 scenes** (long, stage-like); Single-Cam: **40–60+ scenes** (rapid cuts) | 30–45 scenes | Landau Tip 21 |
| **Standing Sets** | **3 Ideal (1 primary + 2 secondary), 5 maximum** | Substantially higher | Blum p. 143 |
| **Structural Unit** | Beats: A-Story (5–9), B-Story (3–5), Thread (3–6) | Scenes & Act Outs | Smith pp. 117–119 |
| **Joke Density** | **2–4 hard laughs per page** | Humor as relief | Smith p. 39, 83 |
| **Stakes** | Small but life-or-death to character (**"Tremendous Trifle"**) | Survival / Institutions | Landau; Smith |
| **Silence** | Multi-cam silence = dead air; Single-cam silence = cringe comedy | Silence as tension | Landau Tip 21 |
| **Character Arc** | Episodic reset (*"learns lesson... resets next week"*); long-term expansion | Serialized change | Smith; Lloyd |

### Core Axioms
1. **The Engine is Relational Density, Not External Plot**: Producers scrutinize the sheer proximity and intimacy between regular characters. Comedic longevity relies on how reliably those personalities clash.
2. **Multi-Camera vs. Single-Camera Represents Two Distinct Arts**: Multi-camera functions like live theater (few scenes, long takes, blocking characters entering and exiting a fixed proscenium); single-camera operates like a mini-feature film (fluid cutting, visual gags, cinematic rhythm). Have writing samples of both.

## II. Premise-Driven Comedy: The Three-Tier Method

```
[Tier 1: Premise] ➔ Seed ongoing predicaments & character mix
     ⬇
[Tier 2: Sequence & Scene] ➔ Compound predicaments & stir unnatural alliances
     ⬇
[Tier 3: Dialogue & Action] ➔ Harvest setup/punchline mechanics & physical business
```

### 2.1 Tier 1: The Four Foundations of a Comedy Premise
1. **Predicaments**: An ongoing dilemma is a self-sustaining joke machine. While a temporary scene complication lasts only a few pages, a persistent predicament embedded into the premise generates endless humor until resolved.
2. **Character Mix**: Conflicting worldviews forced into permanent contact.
3. **Style of Comedy**: High farce, wry dramedy, deadpan cringe, or slapstick.
4. **Casting Dynamic**: Physical contrasts, vocal cadences, and idiosyncratic energies.

### 2.2 The 9 Classic Predicaments & 6 Character Mixes
- **The 9 Predicaments**:
  1. *The Big Lie* (a deception requiring ever-larger lies to sustain).
  2. *The Secret Mission* (achieving an objective without letting others discover it).
  3. *The Trap / Incarceration* (characters physically or socially stuck together).
  4. *The Fish Out of Water* (a character forced into an alien environment).
  5. *Role Reversal* (boss becomes subordinate; parent becomes child).
  6. *Mistaken Identity* (one character assumed to be someone else).
  7. *The Dilemma of Loyalty* (torn between two competing, irreconcilable obligations).
  8. *The Impossible Task* (forced to achieve an absurd goal under a deadline).
  9. *The Domino Effect* (a minor slip spiraling out of control).
- **The 6 Character Mixes**:
  1. *The Odd Couple* (neat vs. slob; cynic vs. optimist).
  2. *The Comic Triangle* (two rivals competing for the approval of a third).
  3. *The Innocent & The Cynic* (wide-eyed naivety meeting worldly corruption).
  4. *The Anchor & The Maniacs* (one sane straight-man surrounded by lunatics).
  5. *The Mirror Pair* (identical flaws escalating each other's neuroses).
  6. *The Dysfunctional Family / Tribe* (bound by blood or employment, unable to separate).

### 2.3 Tier 2: Compounding Sequences and Scenes
- **Compound Story Predicaments**: Do not merely double problems; compound them exponentially (*Big Lie + Unnatural Alliance + Ticking Clock*).
- **Stir Up Unnatural Alliances**: Force two characters who despise each other to team up temporarily to solve an urgent crisis. The audience anticipates both the resolution of the plot and the inevitable explosion of the alliance.
- **Comedy Climax**: Escalate toward an explosive comedy set piece in Act II or Act III.
- **Foibles Over Villains**: The most sustainable comedic crises stem directly from the protagonist's own blind spots, vanities, or petty obsessions.

## III. Joke Mechanics (Tier 3)

The classical joke model:
$$\text{Setup (Straight Line / Tension)} \longrightarrow \text{Punchline (Sudden Twist / Release)}$$
The setup introduces a topic and creates anticipation in Direction A; the punchline resolves it unexpectedly from Direction B.

### 3.1 The Five Rules of Setups
1. **Plausible & Grounded**: The setup must sound completely natural. If the setup feels strange or forced, the audience anticipates the trap.
2. **Two-Stage Architecture**: A great setup contains:
   $$\text{Topic} + \text{1–2 Beats of Development (Complication / Contrast)}$$
   Without the development phase, there is insufficient tension, and the punchline feels like mundane information.
3. **Spatial Distribution**: A setup can be planted pages earlier or whispered across multiple characters before the punchline strikes.
4. **Never Telegraph**: Never prompt the punchline with an unnecessary clarifying question.
5. **Shared Cultural Suffering**: Universal human annoyances (airports, bureaucracy, dating, aging) provide instant, implicit setups.

### 3.2 The Six Rules of Punchlines
1. **Last Things Last**: The operative punch word must land on the very last syllable of the sentence. *(Example: "I believe he thinks it's a newspaper for people who work. Daily." beats "It's a daily newspaper for workers.")*
2. **Don't Step on the Joke**: Stop speaking immediately after delivering the punchline. Continuing dialogue stomps on the audience's laughter.
3. **The Topper**: Stacking 1–2 escalating punchlines onto an existing setup.
   - *Rule*: Never top more than twice (a third topper reveals the writer's strings).
   - *Rule*: Dramedies rarely use toppers; they break grounded emotional realism.
4. **Running Gags & Callbacks**: Reintroducing a punchline 2–3 times across an episode under new narrative contexts. The final iteration should feature an ironic reversal that delivers the biggest laugh.
5. **When Stuck, Fix the Setup**: If a punchline falls flat, stop rewriting the punchline. Go back and sharpen the setup's contrast or stakes.
6. **Go for the Literal**: Literal interpretations of figurative idioms work exceptionally well when delivered by either the village idiot or the hyper-intellectual.

### 3.3 Ten Comedic Nuances (Character Vocal Prints)
Characters should possess distinct comedic weapons rather than drawing from an undifferentiated pool:
1. *Understated Response* (deadpan minimalism in the face of disaster).
2. *Exaggerated Response* (catastrophizing minor inconveniences).
3. *Dry Irony* (saying the exact opposite of reality with zero inflection).
4. *Nervous Babbling* (talking uncontrollably when anxious, revealing secrets).
5. *Brutal Candor* (pulling no punches; zero social filter).
6. *Space-Shot POV* (living in a completely different reality).
7. *High-Speed Wordplay* (puns, rhyming, rapid linguistic acrobatics).
8. *Double Entendre* (innocent statements loaded with secondary meaning).
9. *Self-Delusional Grandeur* (boasting moments before public failure).
10. *Petty Vindictiveness* (obsessively settling meaningless social scores).

### 3.4 Density Standards
- **Standard**: **2–4 hard laughs per script page**.
- **The Red-Pen Audit**: Read your draft and circle every genuine laugh with a red pen. If a page has fewer than two confirmed laughs, it must be rewritten.
- **The Three Laugh Tiers**: *Smile $\to$ Chuckle $\to$ Belly Laugh*. Physical slapstick produces belly laughs; dry wit produces smiles and chuckles. Balance both.

## IV. Half-Hour Narrative Architecture

### 4.1 The Beat Sheet (1 Page)
- **A-Story**: 5–9 major beats.
- **B-Story (Subplot)**: 3–5 beats.
- **Thread / C-Story**: 3–6 brief beats.
- *Anatomy of a Beat*: Every beat must represent an **action and a reaction** that forces the protagonist to change tactics: *"Dwight discovers the hidden tape recorder $\to$ claims it belongs to corporate security to save face."*

### 4.2 Three-Act Structure & Page Distribution (Single-Cam 30p)
- **Act I (Pages 1–5)**: The inciting incident hits within the first 2 pages; the protagonist selects an absurd or ill-advised plan.
- **Act II (Pages 5–22)**: Escalating obstacles; compounding complications; ends with the Midpoint Reversal and the All-Hope-Is-Lost revelation.
- **Act III (Pages 22–28)**: High-speed resolution; the public comedy climax; the protagonist faces the music and learns a minor lesson.
- **Tag (Pages 29–30)**: A standalone comedic button returning the characters to stasis.

> [!NOTE]
> **Page-Count Choice Rule**:
> - Multi-Camera 2-Act Broadcast: Act I (10–12 mins), Act II (10–12 mins).
> - Single-Camera 3-Act Spec: Act I (1–5p), Act II (15–20p), Act III (5–10p).
> - Pilot Exception: Pilot episodes must build the world and introduce character DNA; Act I will naturally run 6–8 pages.

### 4.3 Neil Landau's Seven-Step Sitcom Formula
1. **The Protagonist's Daily Stasis**: Established with an immediate comic flaw.
2. **The Tremendous Trifle**: A seemingly small disruption that the protagonist treats as a catastrophic crisis.
3. **The Unwise Choice**: The protagonist rejects the simple, honest solution and chooses an elaborate, deceptive shortcut.
4. **The Escalation Ladder (Hound Up a Tree)**: Every effort to fix the mistake creates two worse problems.
5. **The Midpoint Reversal / Worst-Case Climax**: The deception collapses publicly in front of the worst possible audience.
6. **The Moral Equation**: The protagonist accepts accountability (or shifts blame) and achieves a bittersweet, messy compromise.
7. **The Tag Button**: The status quo is restored, but with a lingering, hilarious consequence.

## V. Character Architecture: Who Belongs in the Machine?

### 1. The Comic Protagonist
- **Flawed but Driven**: Characters do not need to be likable, but they must possess intense passion, urgency, and vulnerability (*"In comedy, it's usually the troublemakers we like best"*). Michael Scott is narcissistic and insensitive, but his desperate need for family and his unmatched sales skill make him deeply empathetic.
- **Zero Self-Edit Button**: Sitcom characters act on petty impulses that normal people suppress.

### 2. The Proximity Imperative
The premise **must physically or socially trap the characters together**:
- A small regional paper company office (*The Office*).
- An isolated bar (*Cheers*).
- A community college study group (*Community*).
- A cramped family apartment (*Modern Family*).
When regular characters are forced into constant proximity, a conflict over how toilet paper is hung becomes an entire episode.

### 3. Complementary Character Traits (Kam Miller)
Situations are never inherently funny; characters are. Modern comedy favors **complementary, flawed-but-well-intentioned traits** over mean-spirited malice.
- *The Modern Family Benchmark*: Every character is introduced with positive intent; any two characters paired together generate natural friction; everyone's perspective has partial validity.

## VII. Direct Address & Device Comedy (*Fleabag*)

Breaking the fourth wall (`(to camera)`) is **not a stylistic gimmick; it is a psychological defense mechanism**. In *Fleabag*, the protagonist woke up feeling an unbearable void, so she put on a performance for an imaginary audience.

### The Mechanics of Fourth-Wall Intimacy
1. **The Three-Beat Setup with Opponent Reversal**: The protagonist pitches a flattering adjective to the camera, delivers proof, and then an opponent immediately punctures it.
2. **Tell Jokes to the Camera, Tell Truth to Humans**: The camera receives cynical one-liners, irony, and defense mechanisms. Only in the climactic emotional crisis does the protagonist speak genuine vulnerability without looking at the lens.
3. **The Device as a Season Arc**:
   - *Season 1*: The camera is an accomplice. At the climax, the protagonist attempts to hide, and the camera hunts her down, forcing her trauma into the light.
   - *Season 2*: A secondary character (The Priest) notices the device (*"Where did you just go?"*). He forces her out of dissociation until she voluntarily waves goodbye to the camera in the finale.
4. **The Pain-Laugh-Pain Pattern**: High-intensity dramatic scenes must be cut with sharp, awkward comedy before returning to devastation.

## VIII. Dramedy & "Comedy Carrying Tragedy"

### 1. The Dramedy Boundary (Pamela Douglas)
Dramedy is characterized by serialized emotional arcs, deep character flaws, and wry, observational irony—**not mechanical joke-to-joke rhythms**.
- *The Douglas Dictum*: No series running longer than three minutes can survive on joke-to-joke gag writing alone.
- *The Tragedy Rule*: The closer a story moves toward catastrophe, the more vital comedy becomes. Shakespeare invariably introduced the gravediggers and fools before the bloodbath.

### 2. The *Succession* Principles (Jesse Armstrong)
- **Use Jokes to Absorb Devastating Confessions**: When Kendall confesses to manslaughter, his siblings do not offer sentimental therapy; they crack brutal, dark jokes (*"At worst you're a manslaughter-er"*). *Cruel family humor is the only language of love they know.*
- **The Comedy Runner as Narrative Thermostat**: Secondary comedic threads (Greg's absurd legal schemes) run parallel to high-stakes corporate warfare, keeping the tone buoyant before snapping into genuine tension.

## IX. Sitcom Pilots & Series Bible

- **Write a Typical-Episode Pilot by Default**: Premise pilots (depicting the origin event that created the situation) often fail because they do not demonstrate what Episode 45 looks like on an ordinary Tuesday. Executives prefer a typical episode that drops us straight into the comedy engine with minimal exposition.
- **The 10–15 Page Series Format**:
  - The Pitch Hook & Engine.
  - Character Portfolios (written with distinctive comedic voices).
  - The Primary Standing Sets & Proximity Engine.
  - 12–15 Episodic Springboards proving the premise has **100-episode legs**.

## X. The Comedy Writers' Room & Punch-Up Dynamics

- **Room Archetypes**:
  - *The Schur Room (Parks & Rec)*: Break stories collectively; outline together; assign drafting to individuals; run rapid punch-ups.
  - *The Kaling Room*: Focuses on individual authorial voices and pitch documents.
  - *The Rae Room (Insecure)*: Collective room drafting accompanied by internal room table reads.
  - *The Levy Room (Schitt's Creek)*: Tight 4-person room; turns narrative beats directly into full dialogue before handing off scenes.
- **Alts & The Candy Bag (Greg Daniels & Neil Landau)**: During punch-up sessions, writers generate 10–20 alternate jokes (*alts*) for weak lines. Unused brilliant jokes are saved in the project's *Candy Bag* for future episodes.
- **"Pack the Sausage"**: Never hoard your best jokes for later episodes. Put your finest material into the current draft.

## XI. Table of Professional Disagreements

| Topic | Perspective A | Perspective B | Operational Guidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Can Comedy Be Taught?** | Comedy is pure innate instinct; you are born funny or you aren't (Landau). | Comedy is an engineered discipline of contrast, rhythm, and setup mechanics (Smith). | **Learn the mechanics to establish a high structural floor**; calibrate your comedic taste in the room. |
| **Room Writing vs. Solo Drafting** | The entire room should write and punch every line together (Issa Rae). | Collective drafting yields diminishing returns; assign scripts to solo drafts (Michael Schur). | Break stories collectively; let solo writers draft the emotional spine; bring the room back for the comedy punch-up. |
| **Table Read Objectives** | Test every joke; cut lines that fail to generate audible laughs (Smith, Lloyd). | Test structural momentum and character truth; ignore flat lines (Dan Levy). | If the humor relies on rapid dialogue, test the laughs; if it relies on deadpan acting and edits, test the structure. |
| **Act-Out Cliffhangers** | Every act must conclude on a massive commercial cliffhanger (Smith). | End acts on quiet, earned emotional revelations (Dan Levy). | Use sharp plot turns for broadcast TV with ad breaks; use character-driven turns for commercial-free streaming. |

## XII. Master Diagnostic Checklist

### Premise & Concept Level
- [ ] Is the premise an inherent joke-producing machine, or are jokes being forced into dialogue?
- [ ] Is the central predicament persistent or will it expire after three scenes?
- [ ] Are any two regular characters providing the exact same comic function?
- [ ] Does this episode expand the series premise without breaking established canon?
- [ ] Is there an inescapable proximity engine forcing the characters together?

### Structure & Pacing
- [ ] Does the A-Story contain 5–9 distinct action-reaction beats?
- [ ] Is Act I lean and fast (1–5 pages in a 30p single-cam script)?
- [ ] Does the episode build toward a major comedy climax in Act II or III?
- [ ] Are there clean act-break reversals?
- [ ] Are you within the standing set budget (3 ideal, 5 maximum)?

### Joke Mechanics & Punch-Up
- [ ] **The Red-Pen Audit**: Are there 2–4 confirmed laughs on every single page?
- [ ] Do setups feature a clear topic and a distinct development beat?
- [ ] Does the punch word land on the absolute final syllable of the sentence?
- [ ] Did you avoid topping more than twice?
- [ ] Does the running gag return 2–3 times with an ironic reversal on the finish?
- [ ] Are characters restricted to their signature comedic nuances?
- [ ] Have you replaced explanatory dialogue with visual takes, physical business, or reactions?

### Format Discipline
- [ ] Is the script correctly styled as Single-Cam (28–30p, mixed case) or Multi-Cam (45–50p, double-spaced, capitalized)?
- [ ] Are sluglines, act tags (`END OF ACT ONE`), and transitions properly designated?

## XIII. Step-by-Step Comedy Writing Workflow

```
[0. Show Immersion] ➔ [1. What-If Springboards] ➔ [2. One-Page Beat Sheet]
       ➔ [3. Premise Comedy Check] ➔ [4. The Detailed Outline]
       ➔ [5. Rapid First Draft] ➔ [6. Red-Pen Joke Audit] ➔ [7. The Punch-Up Table]
```

1. **Step 0: Show Immersion**: Transcribe 3 scenes of the target show to internalize its rhythm and joke density.
2. **Step 1: The What-If Springboard**: Generate 10–15 brief premises based on a regular character's emotional blind spot.
3. **Step 2: The Beat Sheet**: Construct the 5–9 A-Story beats and 3–5 B-Story beats.
4. **Step 3: Premise Check**: Identify the core predicament and unnatural alliances.
5. **Step 4: The Outline (5–7 Pages)**: Draft the scene-by-scene outline. Verify the story holds at the 2/3 mark.
6. **Step 5: The Rapid Draft (7–10 Days)**: Write quickly without obsessing over perfection to capture momentum.
7. **Step 6: The Red-Pen Audit**: Count the jokes per page; fix setups where laughs are thin.
8. **Step 7: The Punch-Up**: Brainstorm 3–5 alts for every critical joke and pick the sharpest punchline.

## XIV. Applied Training Drills

1. **The Red-Pen Dissection**: Take an authentic, Emmy-winning half-hour script. Mark every laugh with a red pen. Calculate the exact laugh density per page and categorize each joke type.
2. **Setup Reconstruction**: Select three jokes that fell flat in your writing. Leave the punchlines alone—completely rewrite the setups by increasing the stakes or sharpening the contrast.
3. **The 5-Minute Device Test**: If using an unusual narrative framing device (direct address, fantasy cutaways), record yourself performing it on a phone for 5 minutes. If it feels grating, abandon it.
4. **The Solo Punch-Up Drill**: Take a 3-page scene written by a peer. Generate 10 alternative punchlines for the primary comedic moments. Filter them down to your top 3.
