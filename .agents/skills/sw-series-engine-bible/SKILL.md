# Series Engine & Master Bible Architecture (sw-series-engine-bible)

> **Output Language:** English (or matching user query language). All terminology anchors to original craft terms (see [terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)).

This skill governs the foundational machinery of television and episodic streaming creation:
1. **The Series Engine:** The inexhaustible dynamic conflict that generates weekly drama.
2. **The Character Web:** The irreconcilable interpersonal relationships that automatically manufacture friction.
3. **Pilot Strategy:** Choosing the optimal entry point (Premise vs. Ongoing vs. Hybrid).
4. **The Pitch Pipeline:** Moving from Logline → Series Presentation → Treatment → Master Series Bible.

---

## 1. What is a Series Engine?

- **The Franchise Equation (William Rabkin):**
  - `Franchise = Concept + Conflict + Theme + Story Pattern`
  - A high concept is merely a container (e.g., plane crash on an island). A true franchise requires a renewable conflict engine driven by a deep thematic debate.
- **Theme Must Be an Arguable Polarized Proposition:**
  - *"There is no such thing as a one-word theme for a television series."* A one-word theme (*"Family"*, *"Ambition"*) is inert.
  - *The Shield:* How much evil can a good man commit in the pursuit of justice before becoming the very evil he fights?
  - *Mad Men:* A man makes a fortune selling the fantasy that consumerism brings happiness, knowing intimately that it brings none to his own life.
  - *Lost:* Faith versus reason.
- **Theme Must Materialize as Inescapable Human Friction:**
  - Television cannot handle abstract academic debate. The thematic dilemma must be manifested through opposing characters who possess incompatible worldviews.
- **The "99 Stories" Bite Test:**
  - Rapidly pitch five distinct episode stories that naturally emerge from the character web. If you hit a wall after the pilot, the concept is a two-hour feature film masquerading as a series.
- **The Jazz Analogy:** The franchise and character web are the underlying chord changes; individual weekly episodes are the improvised melodic solos.
- **The Tacit Contract (Neil Landau):**
  - The pilot script establishes an unspoken promise with the viewer: every episode will be **fundamentally the same, yet delightfully different**. The network executive's question *"What is the franchise?"* means: **What do these characters actively DO every week?**
- **The Story Pattern:**
  - The repeatable dramatic shape of an episode (e.g., in *Sons of Anarchy*, Jax uncovers a threat to one of his three "families", takes a clandestine action to protect it, which detonates and endangers another family).
- **Engine Pressure Loss Theory (Jesse Armstrong on *Succession*):**
  - Concluding a series should not be dictated by whether characters have run out of dialogue, but by the **brutal materialist plumbing of the plot engine** (corporate acquisitions, legal trials, political campaigns). When the material plot engine loses pressure, the show must conclude.

## 2. Translating the Engine into Active Sentences

- **The Central Question Must Be a Process, Not a Solution:**
  - Films ask: *"Who killed the victim?"* (Solution-oriented).
  - Television asks: *"How will the protagonist keep doing X in a world that constantly does Y?"* (Process-oriented).
  - *Downton Abbey:* How will Lord Grantham preserve his ancestral estate within an evolving economic system designed to dismantle it?
  - *Breaking Bad:* How will a mild-mannered chemistry teacher survive within the lethal drug underworld without destroying his family?
- **Story Landmines:**
  - Seeds of character contradiction and unspoken secrets planted in the pilot's subtext, designed to detonate in future seasons. Every primary character must carry at least one psychological landmine.
- **Suffering is Not Dramatic Conflict:**
  - Passive misery or depression cannot drive an episodic series. Conflict requires **competing desires and active pursuit**. A character's internal contradiction must manifest as two incompatible active goals that actively sabotage each other.
- **The Sweet Spot:** `Sweet Spot = Character Development × Franchise Engine`. A series must deliver the commercial promise of its premise every single week.

## 3. The Dynamic Character Web

- **Externalizing Internal Conflict:**
  - Never trap a character's core dilemma entirely inside their head. Surround them with a circle of characters who externalize competing pressures (e.g., in *Buffy the Vampire Slayer*, her desire for a normal adolescent life is externalized by her mother and high-school friends, while her destiny is demanded by her Watcher).
- **Every Series is a Family Drama (Landau):**
  - Whether bound by biology, military service, corporate survival, or criminal partnership, television ensembles operate under the psychological laws of dysfunctional families.
  - *The Iron Law of Intimacy: The people who love you most know exactly which emotional buttons to push to hurt you the most.*
- **Specialization of Labor and Competing POVs:**
  - Assign distinct professional disciplines, moral thresholds, and coping mechanisms to every ensemble member. If two characters consistently agree on tactics, merge them.
- **Grounded Superpowers that Double as Lethal Liabilities:**
  - A character's greatest strength must simultaneously be their most destructive flaw (e.g., Walter White’s staggering genius is inseparable from his monstrous pride).
- **Villains Are the Heroes of Their Own Story:**
  - Authentic antagonists believe their actions are righteous, entitled, or necessary for survival.
- **The Three Requisites of a Sustainable Character Web:**
  1. A shared operational arena or collective bond.
  2. Pre-installed, incompatible character traits that ensure friction.
  3. A situational trap that makes it impossible for them to simply walk away.

## 4. Pilot Strategies & Entrance Points

1. **Premise Pilot:**
   - Depicts the transformative "Day One" inciting incident that shatters the old world and establishes the new status quo (e.g., *Lost*, *Breaking Bad*, *The Walking Dead*).
   - *Requirement: Must not spend the entire hour on backstory. The second half of the pilot must demonstrate the repeatable weekly story pattern.*
2. **Ongoing / Midstream Pilot:**
   - Enters a world where the franchise machinery is already fully operational (e.g., *The Sopranos*, *The Office*, *Succession*). The pilot represents a typical episode operating at maximum pressure.
3. **Hybrid Pilot:**
   - Launches in medias res with a high-stakes crisis, then executes a targeted flashback to ground the origin before driving forward.
- **The Golden Rule of Entry:** Enter the character's life as late as possible in their historical timeline, but enter the dramatic crisis as early as possible on page one.

## 5. Television Typologies & Story-Types (Peter Oberg)

### Series Formats
- **Procedural (Closed-Ended):** Self-contained cases resolved within 45 minutes; character relationships reset cleanly.
- **Continuous Serial:** A sprawling narrative tapestry where every episode directly cascades into the next.
- **Serial-Procedural Hybrid:** The dominant modern standard: self-contained weekly cases (A-Story) paired with multi-season serialized character arcs (B-Story).
- **Limited Series (Anthology):** 4 to 8 closed episodes exploring an expansive narrative canvas.

### The Three Season Story-Types
- **Plot-Led:** The overarching seasonal obstacle originates from external forces (an invading cartel, an infectious outbreak).
- **Character-Led:** The crisis is driven by the protagonist’s internal psychological decay or personal choices.
- **Theme-Led:** An ensemble examination of an overarching societal institution (e.g., *The Wire*).

## 6. Documentation: Pitch Deck to Master Series Bible

1. **The Logline:** One crisp, punchy sentence capturing protagonist, flawed setup, primary obstacle, and the weekly engine. Must contain ironclad dramatic irony.
2. **The Series Overview (1–2 Pages):** Establishes tone, setting, thematic arena, and articulates the weekly story springboards.
3. **Character Profiles (3–5 Pages):** Focus on 3D psychology: core wound, secret desire, relational friction vectors, and latent landmines. Avoid superficial appearance checklists.
4. **Pilot Story Beat Sheet (3–4 Pages):** Outlines the pilot act-by-act, tracking escalating act-outs and emotional reversals.
5. **Future Episode Springboards (5–8 Loglines):** Concrete loglines proving the engine’s renewable longevity across Season 1 and beyond.
6. **Multi-Season Roadmap (1–2 Pages):** High-level tentpole trajectories projecting character arcs across future seasons without micromanaging minute details.

## 7. Universal Series Engine Diagnostic Checklist

1. Is the series theme formulated as an arguable, polarized proposition rather than a single word?
2. Does the core dramatic question articulate an ongoing **process** (*"How will they continue to..."*) rather than a simple whodunit solution?
3. Can you pitch five distinct, compelling episode springboards that don't repeat identical conflicts?
4. Is the internal conflict of the protagonist externalized across a circle of competing supporting characters?
5. Does every primary character carry a planted psychological **landmine** set to detonate later in the season?
6. Does the protagonist possess an undeniable **license to intervene** (legal, medical, moral, or situational agency)?
7. Is the character web bound by a situational trap that prevents them from separating?
8. Does the pilot demonstrate the repeatable weekly **story pattern** before the final credits?
9. Does the series overview avoid summarizing merely the pilot, articulating instead the renewable world engine?
10. If the series is an ongoing serial, can you identify the material plot plumbing that will sustain momentum across multiple seasons?

## 8. Step-by-Step Workflow: From Concept to Master Bible

1. **Arena Immersion & Research:** Investigate the operational realities of the chosen world; identify institutional hypocrisies and procedural details.
2. **Define the Central Thematic Proposition:** Formulate the core philosophical clash.
3. **Design the Core Engine & Springboard:** Establish how cases, crises, or interpersonal conflicts enter the world each week.
4. **Construct the Character Web:** Create the ensemble matrix, locking in relational vectors, mutual needs, and conflicting goals.
5. **Select Pilot Strategy:** Choose Premise, Ongoing, or Hybrid based on world complexity.
6. **Draft 6–8 Episode Springboards:** Test the engine's renewable versatility across diverse situations.
7. **Assemble the Master Bible:** Synthesize Logline, World Overview, Character Profiles, Pilot Treatment, and Season Arc into a cohesive document.
8. **Conduct the Stress Test:** Audit against the 10-point Diagnostic Checklist to eliminate feature-film blind spots.
