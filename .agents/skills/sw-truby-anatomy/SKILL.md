# John Truby's Organic Story Anatomy (sw-truby-anatomy)

> **Output Language:** English (or matching user query language). All terminology anchors back to original craft terms.

This skill synthesizes John Truby's master methodology from *The Anatomy of Story* (2007). Truby's industry role is as a premier "script doctor"—diagnosing structural illnesses in studio screenplays. This methodology constitutes the diagnostic engine itself: **Designing Principle, Seven Key Steps, 22 Building Blocks, Four-Corner Opposition, Moral Argument Chain, and Scene Weave**.

---

## 1. Relationship to the 3-Act Page-Count Paradigm

**Dual systems kept side by side:**
* **Truby's Organic Position:** The seven key steps *"do not represent a mechanical story structure imposed arbitrarily from the outside, like the three-act structure. They exist organically inside the story"* (Ch3). The 22 steps *"never tell you what to write; that is what formula or genre does"* (Ch8).
* **Field / Snyder Structural Position (`sw-story-structure`):** Structure = Page-count map. One page per minute. Inciting incident, Plot Points, Midpoint, and All Is Lost occur at precise page anchors.

**The two are complementary, solving different problems:**
* The page map governs **runtime, pacing, and macro-rhythm**.
* Organic anatomy verifies **whether the structural skeleton genuinely grows out of the protagonist's flaw and psychology**.

**Recommended Workflows (Choose one, do not duplicate):**
1. **Map First, Audit Second (Most Common):** After finalizing the BS2 (Save the Cat) beat board, use this skill to audit middle-act component gaps (Sections 4 & 5) and the moral argument chain (Section 7).
2. **Skeleton First, Page Map Second:** Start with the designing principle and the 7 key steps to forge the organic spine, then map into exact page numbers via `sw-story-structure`.

## 2. The Designing Principle

* **Definition:** The overarching storytelling strategy expressed in a single sentence. *"The designing principle is what organizes the story as a whole. It is what makes the story original. It is the deeper strategy of how to tell this premise"* (Ch2).
* **Premise vs. Designing Principle:**
  * **Premise:** Specific and concrete—what happens in the plot (*"A mafia son takes over the family business"*).
  * **Designing Principle:** Abstract and procedural—how it is uniquely told (*"Show the transformation of an idealistic war hero into a cold, ruthless mafia don through the ritual succession of American capitalism"*).
  * **Formula:** `Designing Principle = Story Process + Unique Execution Method`.
* **Three Levels of Foundation (Each does one specific job):**
  1. **Egri Premise (`sw-premise-theme`):** Character trait + leads to + climax/result (Moral proof obligation).
  2. **McKee Controlling Idea:** Value + Cause (The ultimate meaning of the climax).
  3. **Truby Designing Principle:** Formal execution strategy (The structural vessel).
* **Common Designing Principle Sources:**
  * A metaphorical journey (*Heart of Darkness* / *Apocalypse Now*: Journey upriver into human darkness).
  * A central symbol (The letter "A" in *The Scarlet Letter*; the white whale in *Moby Dick*).
  * A unit of time (*Meet Me in St. Louis*: Structured through the four seasons).
  * Multiple narrators (*Citizen Kane*: Multiple perspectives investigating a single missing word).
  * Inverted social process (*Tootsie*: A male chauvinist must live as a woman to learn how to be a real man).

## 3. The Seven Key Steps & The Accordion Principle

**The Seven Key Steps (The Organic Spine — Ch3):**

| # | Step | Essence | Core Craft Alignment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Weakness & Need** | The hero is crippled by a fatal flaw; must satisfy an inner need to live authentically. | Lisa Cron's Misbelief, Hicks' Inner Need, Egri 3D |
| 2 | **Desire** | The visible, concrete, tangible outer goal the hero pursues. | The Story Goal / "Want" |
| 3 | **Opponent** | The force competing for the exact same goal, with opposing values. | Antagonist (`sw-character-conflict`) |
| 4 | **Plan** | The specific strategic set of actions to defeat the opponent. | Protagonist Strategy |
| 5 | **Battle / Climax** | The final decisive conflict determining who achieves the goal. | McKee's Story Climax |
| 6 | **Self-Revelation** | The hero realizes their fatal error and how they mistreated others. | Anagnorisis / Character Arc Payoff |
| 7 | **New Equilibrium** | The world stabilizes at a higher or changed moral state. | Resolution / New Status Quo |

**Two Crucial Rules:**
1. The hero **must not be aware of their true need at the beginning**; they only realize it during the Self-Revelation.
2. Need must have two layers: **Psychological Need** (hurts only the self) and **Moral Need** (their flaw is actively hurting others). Stories with only psychological needs lack a moral engine.

**The Accordion Principle (Ch8):**
A story can expand or contract between 7 and 22+ steps. A short film or commercial can operate on the 7 core steps; a feature film requires 22 steps. Multi-strand ensemble stories require each storyline to independently run through the 7 key steps.

## 5. The Five Critical Mid-Act Components (Weak Spots in Beat Sheets)

### 1. Opponent / Fake-Ally Opponent (Step 8)
An enemy posing as a friend. Because the hero trusts them, the fake ally is positioned at the hero's blind spot to attack their core weakness. Provides a **double revelation**: the hero discovers the betrayal, while the audience often learns it earlier. (Example: Tessio and Carlo in *The Godfather*).

### 2. The Revelation & Decision Sequence (Steps 9, 15, 17)
Every major revelation in Act 2 must be tethered to a **decision** that modifies the hero's desire or tactic. If a revelation does not alter behavior, it is merely decorative exposition.
* *First Revelation:* Shifts the hero from reactive to active pursuit.
* *Second Revelation:* Deepens the hero's obsessive drive into dangerous moral territory.
* *Third Revelation:* Eliminates all alternatives, forcing the hero into the final crucible.

### 3. The Attack by Ally (Step 13)
The dramatization of the moral argument. As the hero compromises their ethics to win, **the closest ally confronts them directly**. The hero rationalizes (*"I'm doing this for our family!"*). This scene prevents the hero's moral decline from feeling like passive drift.

### 4. The Audience Revelation (Step 16)
Dramatic irony: the audience learns a critical secret before the protagonist does. Suspense shifts from *"What happens next?"* to *"When and how will they find out, and what will it cost them?"*

### 5. The Moral Decision (Step 21)
Occurs immediately after the climax. The hero must make a tangible choice between **two concrete actions** to prove their self-revelation was genuine, not just sentimental talk.

## 6. Four-Corner Opposition

Great stories move beyond simple protagonist vs. antagonist binary conflict.
* **Structure:** A rectangle with the **Hero**, the **Main Opponent**, and at least **Two Secondary Opponents** occupying the four corners.
* **Core Principles:**
  1. **Each opponent attacks the hero's weakness in a distinct way.**
  2. **Secondary conflict:** Opponents also clash with each other, multiplying dramatic friction.
  3. **Conflicting Value Systems:** Give each corner a distinct set of values with positive and negative expressions (e.g., *Unyielding Justice / Ruthless Cruelty* vs. *Compassionate Mercy / Weak Indecision*).
  4. **Push characters to the extremes of the corners** to maximize contrast.

### Four-Corner Worksheet:
| Corner | Character | Method of Attacking Hero's Flaw | Core Value Set (+ and - forms) | Conflict with Other Corners |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Corner 1 (Hero)** | Protagonist | Vulnerable to specific attack | Central Moral Value | Opposes all 3 corners |
| **Corner 2 (Main Opponent)** | Primary Antagonist | Direct frontal assault on weakness | Shadow inversion of hero's value | Clashes with Secondary Opponents |
| **Corner 3 (Fake-Ally Opponent)** | Secret Enemy | Subversive, smiling betrayal | Deceptive / Opportunistic value | Undermines both Hero and Antagonist |
| **Corner 4 (Secondary Opponent)** | Ideological Foil | Institutional or philosophical trap | Rigid ideological value | Rejects Hero's and Main Opponent's methods |

## 7. The Moral Argument Chain

A theme should not be preached; it must be dramatized as a progressive chain of choices:
1. **Values:** Hero begins with a functioning set of beliefs.
2. **Moral Weakness:** Hero starts by hurting others in a minor, normalized way.
3. **Moral Need:** Hero must learn how to act properly toward others to truly grow.
4. **First Immoral Action:** Hero takes an ethically questionable shortcut early on.
5. **Desire:** Hero locks onto a goal and crashes headlong into opposing value systems.
6. **Drive:** Escalating competition between hero and opponent.
7. **Immoral Action $\to$ Criticism $\to$ Rationalization:** Hero compromises principles under pressure, is called out by allies, and defends their actions.
8. **Attack by Ally:** Climax of the moral dispute within the camp.
9. **Obsessive Drive:** Hero doubles down, escalating the stakes.
10. **Resolution of the Argument:**
    * *Battle:* Resolves the plot conflict.
    * *Moral Self-Revelation:* Hero recognizes their blindness.
    * *Moral Decision:* Hero proves the change through action.
    * *Thematic Reveal:* The universal human truth emerges for the audience.

**Balance of Power Rule:** If the hero is too morally pure, no genuine moral mistake is possible. If the opponent is pure comic-book evil, the hero is merely a victim, and the moral engine stalls. Both must possess compelling, flawed humanity.

## 8. Ghost & Story World

* **The Ghost:** A traumatic past event that continually haunts the hero—an unhealed open wound. The ghost is the hero's internal opponent (*"Desire pushes them forward; the ghost pulls them back"*).
* **The Story World:** The physical setting must manifest and aggravate the hero's psychological weakness. If the hero is enslaved by doubt, the world should feel claustrophobic and oppressive.

---

## 9. Scene Weaving

The final step before writing the screenplay draft. A scene list where every scene satisfies three criteria:
1. **Single Action:** Summarized in 1–2 active sentences.
2. **Structural Step Tag:** Tagged with its corresponding Truby step (e.g., *Desire, Plan, Drive, Attack by Ally, Apparent Defeat*).
3. **Storyline Number:** In multi-plot or ensemble scripts, track storyline A, B, C, D.

---

## 10. Diagnostic Checklist (Ten Questions for Sagging Middle Acts)

When Act 2 feels episodic or loses momentum, check:
1. **Is there a continuous Drive?** Or just disconnected events happening to a passive hero?
2. **Is there a Fake-Ally Opponent** working from the inside?
3. **Are there 3 distinct Revelations and Decisions?** Does each revelation force a new choice and alter motivation?
4. **Does the Opponent have their own proactive Plan?**
5. **Does the Hero commit an Immoral Action under pressure, followed by Rationalization?**
6. **Does the Ally attack the Hero's corrupt methods?**
7. **Is there an Audience Revelation (dramatic irony) creating suspense?**
8. **Is there a Gate / Gauntlet / Visit to Death before the final battle?**
9. **Is there a Moral Decision (action-based proof) following the self-revelation?**
10. **Is the Four-Corner Opposition fully populated with clashing value systems?**
