# Scene & Sequence Craft (sw-scene-craft)

> **Output Language:** English (or matching user query language). All terminology anchors to original craft terms (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)).

A scene is both the smallest expressive unit of dramatic storytelling and a miniature complete story in itself. This skill governs every decision required to conceive, structure, and write a cinematic scene: **why here and now, what value is wagered on the table, who wants what and through what tactic, how beats build to a turning point, how props and physical behavior do heavy dramatic lifting, how suspense and delay are engineered, and how to enter late and exit early.**

---

## 1. What is a Scene?

- **Robert McKee’s Definition:** An action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that turns the value-charged condition of a character’s life on at least one value with a degree of perceptible significance.
  - **No Scene Without a Value Turn:** Mark the value polarity (`+` or `-`) at the start and at the end. If the charge remains identical, ask: *"Why does this scene exist?"* If it only conveys background exposition, delete it and weave the information into an active scene.
  - A couple’s breakup that roams across bedroom, kitchen, garage, and highway is still a single dramatic scene if it constitutes one unified dramatic movement.
- **Mei Feng’s Value Test:** When setting a scene, the only mandatory question is: *What specific human value is placed on the table, and how does it reverse or deepen by the end?* Secondary subplot values may be woven in, but the central polarity must turn decisively through character choices under conflict.
- **Syd Field:** A scene is where the story happens.
  - Two parameters: **Place and Time**. Change either, and you have a new scene.
  - Determine the **Dramatic Purpose** first (the single essential piece of story information or relationship change), then select the setting, then discover the internal components (the environment, bystanders, ambient friction).
- **Neill Hicks’ Two-Job Rule:** Every scene must simultaneously accomplish two tasks:
  1. Provide new information that advances the external plot.
  2. Reveal new dimensions of the protagonist’s true character.
  *Scenes where nothing happens (e.g., staring out a hotel window, shaving, routine travel) must be excised.*
- **Why Here and Now? (Lu Jun / Lao She):** Setting is not just a slugline. Why must this encounter happen in this specific environment rather than anywhere else? To dramatize a cook, don't leave him comfortably cooking in a kitchen; place him in a desert rail-laying crew or behind a chaotic battlefield trench when supplies have run dry. Setting dictates pressure.

## 2. Five Steps of Scene Design (Robert McKee)

1. **Establish the Conflict:** Who is driving the scene? What is their tangible scene objective? What does the opposing force want? (The opposing desires must be directly colliding, not tangential).
2. **Note the Opening Value Polarity:** Label the starting emotional/moral charge (`+` or `-`).
3. **Break Down into Beats:**
   - A **Beat** is an exchange of behavior in action/reaction.
   - Label beats with active gerund phrases capturing the subtext (*"Pleading for mercy" / "Stonewalling with icy indifference"*).
   - A new beat occurs only when character behavior or tactics shift significantly.
4. **Note the Closing Value Polarity:** Compare it to the opening charge. If there is no shift, the scene is an eventless placeholder.
5. **Audit Beats for the Turning Point:**
   - Verify that beats compound tension until the value charge flips at the **Turning Point**.
   - A turning point is created only by **Action or Revelation**.
   - Produces a four-fold effect: Surprise → Heightened Curiosity → Insight → New Direction.

### Subtext and Psychology in the Scene
- **Scene Intention vs. Super-Intention:**
  - *Scene Intention:* What the character wants right now in this room.
  - *Super-Intention:* The unconscious, primal life-drive (often contradicting their surface behavior).
  - *Background Desires:* The social and moral constraints that dictate what a character cannot say or do.
- **The Four Diseases of a Weak Scene:**
  1. *Lacking Conflict:* Desires do not directly oppose each other.
  2. *Repetitive Cycling:* Characters argue in circles using different words for the exact same tactical beat.
  3. *Imbalanced Turning Point:* The turn arrives too early (leaving dead time) or too late (rushed and unearned).
  4. *On-the-Nose Dialogue:* Characters openly state their exact feelings and subtext, destroying emotional credibility.

## 3. Scene Pacing, Entrances, and Exits

- **Enter Late, Leave Early (William Goldman / Neill Hicks):**
  - Never enter a scene until the final possible second before conflict ignites.
  - **Exit before the conflict is comfortably resolved.** Leaving the scene on a suspended, unresolved note creates irresistible momentum that pulls the audience into the next scene.
  - Apply this to dialogue as well: cut greetings, pleasantries, and ordering at restaurants; cut directly to the dramatic confrontation.
- **Rhythm & Runtime Dynamics:**
  - Feature films average 40–60 scenes, averaging 2 to 3 minutes each.
  - Contrast tempos: follow an intense, sprawling four-minute confrontation with a punchy one-minute visual transition.
  - As the story accelerates toward an act climax, tighten scene lengths to increase velocity and earn the right to pause.
- **The Principle of Transitions:**
  - The connective tissue between two scenes is a shared or contrasting element:
    - *Character trait:* A petulant child cutting to an immature adult.
    - *Action:* A fist cocking back cutting to a gavel slamming down.
    - *Object:* A glowing cigarette cutting to a factory smokestack.
    - *Audio:* A piercing scream cutting to a screeching train brake.
    - *Idea:* A declaration of undying love cutting to a divorce lawyer's office.
- **The Sequence:**
  - A sequence is a series of 2 to 5 scenes bound together by a single overarching dramatic objective (e.g., the Heist, the Escape, the Interrogation).
  - Establish the sequence concept first (the cup), then fill it with individual scenes (the coffee).

## 4. Action Over Talk (Richard Walter & Wendy Henson)

- **Principle 30: Drama is behavior and physical action.**
- **Principle 31: Any action is superior to no action; action unified with story is best.**
  - Tennessee Williams: *"Make them stand on their heads, make them take off their pants, make them do something—anything, but never let them do nothing."*
- **Magnifying Small Mundane Actions:** Cinema’s greatest superpower is magnifying tiny physical behaviors into profound emotional revelations:
  - A slipping wedding ring rolling across the floor.
  - A father frantically burning a slice of toast while trying to handle a divorce.
  - A detective slowly twisting the cap of an ink pen during an interrogation.
  - *Tiny, everyday physical actions dramatize internal psychological states far better than speeches.*
- **Eliminating Static Staging:**
  - Routinely avoid generic restaurants, coffee shops, cars, and office desks unless they are indispensable to the core action.
  - **The Alternative Location Exercise:** Force yourself to list a dozen alternative locations for an encounter before settling on one (e.g., instead of an office confrontation between two detectives, stage it at an indoor firing range or beside a municipal swimming pool where one must shout over splashing water and smell of chlorine).
- **Wendy Henson’s "Story-Doing":**
  - Convert passive states of emotion into active behavioral verbs:
    - Replace *"I feel devastated"* with active crying, packing a suitcase, or furiously scrubbing a sink.
    - Replace *"You are so rebellious"* with active defiance or subversion.
- **Visual-First Rule:** Before writing a single line of dialogue, challenge yourself: *How can this entire scene be told with absolute clarity if the sound were turned off?*

## 5. Suspense, Delay, and Dramatic Intrigue (Lu Jun / William Archer)

- **Suspense vs. Surprise:**
  - **Surprise (Shock):** A bomb goes off beneath a table without warning (10 seconds of cheap adrenaline).
  - **Suspense (Anticipation):** The audience sees the terrorist plant the bomb set for 1:00 PM; two characters sit down at the table at 12:45 PM and engage in a mundane conversation (15 minutes of unbearable tension).
- **William Archer’s Law:** *"A playwright should never keep secrets from his audience—you cannot keep secrets. The permanent delight of drama lies not in shock, but in anticipation."*
  - Dramatic irony makes the audience God-like spectators watching mortals blindly walk toward peril.
- **The Art of Delay (Retardation):**
  - To jump high, you must bend your knees; to strike hard, you must pull back your fist.
  - When crisis is imminent, deliberately introduce believable micro-delays (a stuck zipper, an uncooperative lock, an interruption by an oblivious neighbor) to stretch tension to its breaking point.
- **Four Sources of Dramatic Intrigue:**
  1. *Intriguing Circumstances:* An impossible or absurd setup played with deadly seriousness.
  2. *Intriguing Characters:* Contradictory behaviors that subvert expectations.
  3. *Intriguing Language:* Wit, irony, and pregnant subtext.
  4. *Intriguing Sensory Details:* Layered observational quirks that create texture.

## 6. Sensory Behavioral Details

- Details are the cellular tissue of character and environment.
- **The Concept of "Conk" (Specificity):**
  - Never write *"a small Midwestern town"*; write *"Springfield, Ohio"*.
  - Never write *"an expensive suit"*; write *"a chocolate-brown double-breasted Armani"*.
  - Never write *"doing arts and crafts"*; write *"gluing popsicle sticks together with trembling fingers"*.
- **Five Functions of a Great Detail:**
  1. Establishes authentic environment instantly.
  2. Accelerates or triggers dramatic conflict.
  3. Reveals unvarnished character psychology.
  4. Crystals into the turning point of the scene.
  5. Manifests the overarching theme in physical form.
- **Discipline Against Indulgence:** Never show off descriptive details for their own sake. A recurring physical detail is justified only if it actively drives the plot or reveals psychological evolution across the story.

## 7. Dramatic Props

- A chosen prop carries more emotional weight than pages of dry dialogue (e.g., Othello’s handkerchief, Nora’s forged bond, the umbrella in *The Legend of the White Snake*).
- **Six Dramatic Functions of Props:**
  1. **Structural Engine:** The object around which the entire plot revolves (a stolen envelope, a hidden flash drive).
  2. **Conflict Catalyst:** An object whose handover or withholding triggers escalating confrontation.
  3. **Character Signature:** A physical habit revealing status or obsession (checking an antique pocket watch).
  4. **Metaphor & Symbol:** A visual embodiment of destiny or inner trauma (a cracked mirror, a dead houseplant).
  5. **Thematic Window:** An everyday artifact highlighting societal injustice.
  6. **Physical Choreography:** An object that grounds staging and gives actors visceral action.
- **The Prop Traversal Chain:**
  - When an important prop travels through a story, each change of possession must be catalyzed by **another character's action** and occur at a decisive value turn.
  - If a prop appears without altering relationships, cut it.
- **The Open Swap (Dramatic Irony):**
  - When a character swaps or steals a crucial prop, show the swap clearly to the audience while keeping the victim in the dark. The subsequent dramatic tension relies on the audience dreading the moment the victim discovers the deception.

## 8. Meaningful Setting (Mise-en-Scène)

- **Eugene O'Neill’s Staging Architecture:**
  - Setting should directly externalize internal psychology (e.g., alternating between open horizons representing wild dreams and claustrophobic interiors representing trapped reality).
  - Soundscapes as living participants: recurring foghorns, ticking clocks, distant gunfire, howling wind that swells with dramatic stakes.
- **Three Criteria for Effective Setting:**
  1. *Serves the Thematic Premise:* Enhances the moral dilemma.
  2. *Excavates Character Truth:* Puts the character in an environment that exposes their vulnerabilities.
  3. *Escalates the Conflict:* The physical environment actively hinders easy communication or escape (narrow elevators, construction scaffolds, loud engine rooms).

## 9. Scene Diagnostic Checklist

1. **Why Here and Now?** Does the location intensify the pressure? Have you explored alternative locations?
2. **What Value is at Stake?** Does the scene turn from positive to negative (`+` to `-`) or negative to positive (`-` to `+`)? If unchanged, is it merely an expository lecture?
3. **Are Desires in Direct Opposition?** Do the characters' scene intentions collide head-on?
4. **Is the Turning Point Clear?** Does the turn occur through a decisive physical action or a game-changing revelation?
5. **Did You Enter Late and Leave Early?** Did you cut right to the conflict and exit before tension dissipated?
6. **Are Characters Trapped in States Instead of Actions?** Are they talking about feelings instead of pursuing tangible goals?
7. **Is Suspense Engineered Through Anticipation?** Did you give the audience the key early to milk dramatic irony?
8. **Are Props and Behavioral Details Doing Heavy Lifting?** Is there a specific physical action that speaks louder than dialogue?
9. **Is the Setting Active?** Does the physical environment impede characters or force them together?
10. **Does the Scene Serve the Macro Sequence?** Does it push the story closer to the act climax?

## 10. End-to-End Workflow: Writing a Master Scene

1. **Identify the Core Dramatic Purpose:** Determine the single story development and character revelation this scene must achieve.
2. **Define the Value Turn:** Record the opening value polarity and the target closing polarity.
3. **Select the Location:** Brainstorm alternative settings; pick the one that maximizes physical pressure and thematic resonance.
4. **Assign Props and Micro-Behaviors:** Identify 1–2 physical props or specific actions that ground the scene in physical reality.
5. **Choose the Suspense Dynamic:** Decide what the audience knows versus what the characters know.
6. **Draft the Beat Sheet:** Outline the sequence of action/reaction gerunds leading up to the turning point.
7. **Cut the Margins:** Shave the entrance to the threshold of conflict; set the exit immediately after the value turn.
8. **Draft the Action Lines:** Use vivid present-tense verbs, concrete nouns, and eliminate screenwriting clichés ("we see", "begins to").
9. **Run the Diagnostic Audit:** Verify that the scene turns, contains authentic conflict, and leaves an indelible visual impression.
