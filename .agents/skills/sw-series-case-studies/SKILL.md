## I. *The West Wing* (Aaron Sorkin): Network 4-Act Craft

Aaron Sorkin's *The West Wing Script Book* collects six solo-authored teleplays (*Pilot, A Proportional Response, In the Shadow of Two Gunmen I & II, 17 People, Two Cathedrals*). As official shooting scripts containing `TEASER`, `ACT ONE` through `ACT FOUR`, and scene numbers, these provide verified measurements of the 42–44 minute broadcast drama:

### 1. Act-by-Act Page Allocation
- **Pilot**: Teaser 8p + Act I 19p / Act II 18p / Act III 12p / Act IV 11p = **68 pages**.
- **A Proportional Response**: Teaser 4p + Act I 14p / Act II 14p / Act III 18p / Act IV 19p = **69 pages**.
- **In the Shadow of Two Gunmen (Part I)**: Teaser 7p + Act I 21p / Act II 14p / Act III 11p / Act IV 14p = **67 pages**.
- **In the Shadow of Two Gunmen (Part II)**: Teaser 2p + Act I 11p / Act II 14p / Act III 16p / Act IV 18p = **61 pages**.
- **17 People (Bottle Episode)**: Teaser 6p + Act I 14p / Act II 10p / Act III 10p / Act IV 18p = **58 pages**.
- **Two Cathedrals (Season Finale)**: Teaser 3p + Act I 13p / Act II 13p / Act III 12p / Act IV 17p = **58 pages**.

### 2. Structural Patterns
- **Teaser Length (2–8 Pages, Median 5)**: Inversely proportional to audience familiarity. The premise pilot requires 8 pages; two-part continuations require only 2–3 pages.
- **Back-Heavy Act Weights**: Except for the pilot (which spends 37 pages building the world), all subsequent episodes feature a dominant, heavy **Act IV (17–19 pages)**. Acts II and III are compressed under pressure.
- **Storyline Count (4–7 Strands)**: Standard episodes weave 4 distinct strands. Bottle episodes expand to 5 strands; season finales deploy 7. *When physical locations are restricted, multiplying storylines provides narrative momentum.*
- **The Procedural Runner**: Each episode features an external crisis (Cuban refugees, military reprisals, FAA airspace threats) that **never claims an act-out**. It exists purely to enforce deadlines and interrupt character drama.
- **The Eight Act-Out Types**:
  1. *The Dilemma Question* (*"What is the proportional response?"*).
  2. *The Status Reversal Line*.
  3. *The Emotional Confession*.
  4. *The Wordless Physical Action*.
  5. *The Prop Freeze-Frame* (the tennis ball hitting the wall).
  6. *The Repetition of Dialogue with Inverted Meaning*.
  7. *The Abrupt Cut Mid-Action*.
  8. *The Verified Behavioral Tell*.

### 3. Transferable Techniques
- **The Hallway Walk-and-Talk as Free Staging**: Stage multi-layered exposition across 3–4 continuous interior transitions (`INT. HALLWAY - CONTINUOUS`), delivering world exposition, character subtext, and comedy in a single camera move.
- **Quantifying the Secret**: Turn an abstract dilemma into a tangible, countable progression (*15 $\to$ 16 $\to$ 17 people who know the secret*).
- **The Sorkin Bottle Principle**: When a studio mandates zero guest stars and zero practical locations, treat the episode like a stage play: force two central characters into a confined space to confront a foundational relationship fracture.

## II. *The Sopranos* (David Chase): Pilot Reticence & The 35-Beat Board

Derived from David Chase's five published shooting scripts (*Pilot, College, The Happy Wanderer, The Knight in White Satin Armor, Pine Barrens*). Lacking commercial broadcast act headers, structures are measured by narrative movements and therapy milestones:

### 1. The 35-Beat Formula
As David Chase outlines: Every episode is broken on a board of roughly **35 narrative beats**:
$$\textbf{A-Story (12–14 beats)} + \textbf{B-Story (12–14 beats)} + \textbf{C-Story (5–6 beats)} + \textbf{D-Runner (2–4 comic beats)}$$
- The A and B stories carry equal dramatic weight.
- The season theme is **never pre-planned**. It is discovered retrospectively in the editing room (*"The relationship with the mother emerged organically across Season 1"*).

### 2. Pilot Reticence & The Therapy Engine
- **The Pilot Weaves Five Simultaneous Lines**: A (Panic attacks / therapy / duck motif) + B (Uncle Junior's restaurant hit) + C (Waste management contract fraud) + D (Mother-son boundary crisis) + E (Season arc mob seeds).
- **The Therapy Room as Structural Framing**: Scene 1 begins in Dr. Melfi's waiting room. The therapy sessions (appearing 11 times in the pilot) provide narrative transitions, voiceover entry points, and psychological framing. Crucially, **the audience always knows more than the therapist**: immediately after witnessing Tony commit vehicular assault, he smiles and tells Melfi: *"Nothing. We had coffee."*
- **Replacing the Therapy Device**: In *College*, the therapy room is absent; it is replaced by Carmela confessing to Father Phil in her home.
- **The Anti-Climactic Finish**: The pilot does not conclude with an explosion or arrest. Uncle Junior receives his blessing in silence, the family gathers for dinner, and the camera pans across an empty, still pool where the ducks once lived. *The episode concludes on an absence.*

### 3. Transferable Techniques
- **The Irony of the Double Life**: Juxtapose graphic underworld brutality directly against bourgeois domestic complaints (*"Talking helps"*).
- **Subplot Fulfillments Born of Main Plot Catastrophes**: A character's minor wish is granted as the direct byproduct of the protagonist destroying a rival's life.
- **The Three-Refusal Protocol**: A character refuses a dangerous proposition three times; when they finally accept, they impose a specific condition that transfers moral culpability back onto the requester.

## III. *Downton Abbey* Season 2 (Julian Fellowes): 8–12 Strand Weaving

Julian Fellowes's shooting scripts contain **419 extensive craft footnotes** detailing why specific lines were cut, how historical constraints shaped scenes, and how British commercial television (ITV) breaks were managed:

### 1. The 19-Strand Narrative Web
Season 2 tracks 19 distinct character storylines across 8 episodes and a Christmas Special. Single episodes carry **10 to 12 active storylines simultaneously**.
- **Scene Volume**: Standard episodes range from **56 to 77 scenes**; the 90-minute Christmas special commands **110 scenes**.
- **The Ensemble Dinner Rule (Footnote 326)**: In large group gatherings (breakfasts, formal dinners, library meetings), the scene must **advance 6 to 7 storylines simultaneously, often with no more than a single line of dialogue per character**.
- **The Four British Act-Out Types**:
  1. *The Transgressive Social Request* (attempting to bribe a lady's maid).
  2. *The Vocalized Hidden Fact* (*"He is standing"*).
  3. *The Wry Aristocratic Button* (*"I have a cold"*).
  4. *The Physical Shock* (a sudden medical collapse, handcuffs, reading scripture).

### 2. Rules for Major Casualties
- A long-running drama must periodically eliminate core characters to preserve genuine stakes.
- **The Whodunit Principle for Deaths**: The character who dies must be deeply established and beloved (*"The killer must be someone who was in the house the entire time"*). Deliberately plant red herrings to make the audience anticipate the death of a different character.

### 3. Transferable Techniques
- **The Downton Dilemma ("Both Sides Are Right")**: *"A typical Downton argument is one where you are hopefully on both sides."* If one side is transparently villainous and wrong, the dramatic strand is flawed.
- **Institutional Obstacles**: Antagonism is provided by legal, social, and military institutions (illegitimacy laws, military triage codes, divorce decrees). The best antagonists are simply characters who understand the institutional rules better than the heroes.

## IV. *Fleabag* (Phoebe Waller-Bridge): The Half-Hour Streaming Model

*The Scriptures* collects all 12 shooting scripts across two seasons. Each season functions as an immaculate 6-episode novella:

### 1. The 6-Episode Season Matrix (Symmetrical Across Both Seasons)
| Episode | Season 1 Function | Season 2 Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Episode 1** | Establishes the relationship web; the unmentioned loss of Boo. | The family reunion dinner; introducing the Priest; the hidden nosebleed. |
| **Episode 2** | Translating internal grief into active financial / sexual pursuit. | Pursuing the Priest; exploring counseling and institutional limits. |
| **Episode 3** | Midpoint social crisis; first glimpse of true vulnerability. | Assisting the church garden party; the Priest notices her dissociation. |
| **Episode 4** | **The Bottle Episode** (Silent feminist retreat). | **The Bottle Episode** (The confession booth confrontation). |
| **Episode 5** | The false victory / escalating sexual mania. | The physical intimacy breakthrough with the Priest. |
| **Episode 6** | The truth revealed; flash-cut to the road; the camera hunts her down. | The wedding; the bus stop goodbye; waving farewell to the camera. |

### 2. The Direct-Address Device as a Narrative Arc
- **Season 1**: The fourth wall is an accomplice. At the climax, she tries to flee; the camera pursues her into the corner and forces the suicide memory onto the screen.
- **Season 2**: The Priest hears and sees the device (*"Where did you just go?"*). The device is transformed into an in-world symptom of dissociation, concluding when she asks the camera not to follow her down the street.
- **The Single Monologue Discipline**: Across the entire season, grant the lead character **one and only one long dramatic monologue delivered without looking at the camera** (the confession booth). That moment becomes the emotional summit of the series.
- **Visual Sentences**: Plant a 2-second visual memory (Boo standing across the street as traffic passes). Insert that image precisely when the protagonist appears happy to signal that she is broken.

## V. Daniel Calvisi's Minute-by-Minute Pilot Roadmaps

Analyzing primary broadcasts across eight landmark television dramas reveals three universal structural landmarks:
- **The Midpoint**: Lands precisely between **Minutes 24–30**.
- **The Assumption of Power (AOP)**: The protagonist embraces their new agency between **Minutes 36–48**.
- **Declaration of War / All Is Lost**: Strikes at the 4/5 mark (**Minutes 43–52**).

```
[0m Teaser] ➔ [15m Catalyst] ➔ [25m Midpoint] ➔ [38m Assumption of Power] ➔ [48m Climax / War] ➔ [55m Resolution]
```

### Key Pilot Takeaways
- **Scandal (45 mins, Teaser + 5 Acts)**: Front-loaded speed. Both the catalyst and inciting incident occur within the 6-minute teaser.
- **Breaking Bad (57 mins, Teaser + 5 Acts)**: Composed of rapid 2-minute scenes. Act V forms a perfect visual circle by returning to the pants falling from the sky in the teaser.
- **Game of Thrones (60 mins, Teaser + 5 Acts)**: Ensemble worldbuilding. The inciting incident is delayed to Minute 18 (the King arrives at Winterfell). Act breaks are marked by geographic title cards.
- **Mad Men (48 mins, 4 Acts, No Teaser)**: The A-Story (Lucky Strike campaign) concludes at the end of Act II; Acts III and IV focus entirely on character unraveling and the secret suburban family reveal.

## VI. Neil Landau's 47-Series Structural Grid

Neil Landau's taxonomy of 47 major television dramas categorizes series along structural parameters:

### 1. Act Formats
- **Teaser + 4 Acts**: The broadcast gold standard (*The West Wing, Breaking Bad, CSI, Law & Order, The X-Files, ER*).
- **Teaser + 5 Acts**: Paced for higher ad density (*Friday Night Lights, The Walking Dead Season 2+, Parenthood*).
- **6 Acts (No Teaser)**: Commercial medical / legal procedural (*Grey's Anatomy, Scandal*).
- **Continuous / Unmarked Acts**: Premium cable and streaming (*The Sopranos, Deadwood, Game of Thrones, Homeland, House of Cards*).

### 2. Pilot Design Archetypes
- **Premise Pilots (Origin Events)**: *Breaking Bad, Lost, The Walking Dead, 24, American Horror Story*. Essential when the world does not exist prior to the inciting incident.
- **Non-Premise Pilots (Typical-Episode Model)**: *The West Wing, Downton Abbey, CSI, Law & Order, Modern Family*. Drops the audience into an ongoing machine with established routines.

## VII. Kam Miller: Scene Escalation Anatomy

A dramatic television scene consists of five structural elements (Gentile / Miller):
$$\text{Hero's Want} \longrightarrow \text{Obstacle} \longrightarrow \text{Escalations (Minimum 3)} \longrightarrow \text{Decision} \longrightarrow \text{Resolution}$$

### 1. Case Study: *Hannibal* Pilot (Bathroom Scene, 1m 57s, 14 Escalations)
- **Want**: Will Graham wants to understand an anomalous serial killer.
- **Obstacle**: Jack Crawford demands immediate operational conclusions.
- **Escalation Chain**:
  `Bathroom inquiry ➔ Sarcastic deflection ➔ Crawford dismisses humor ➔ Crawford questions Will's sanity ➔ Will shares insight (not a textbook psychopath) ➔ Crawford demands hidden evidence ➔ Crawford reveals victim was untouched.`
- **Decision (Climax of Scene)**: Will defends the killer with intimate psychological empathy (*"The killer kills them quickly and mercifully"*).
- **Resolution**: Crawford recognizes Will's brilliant, terrifying instability and assigns a psychiatrist to monitor him.

### 2. Rules for Scene Craft
- Every scene must contain at least two characters with **diametrically opposed desires**.
- **Action Over Talk**: At least 50% of scene escalations must be physical actions, changes in blocking, or prop manipulation rather than verbal debating.

## VIII. Goldberg & Rabkin: Production Beat Sheets

From *Successful Television Writing*, analyzing authentic studio beat sheets (*Martial Law, seaQuest 2032, Diagnosis Murder*):

### 1. Physical Restraint Devices (*Martial Law*, "Sammo Blammo")
- An explosive device strapped to the protagonist's chest requires a continuous physical action to prevent detonation (a motion sensor triggers if he stops moving).
- **The Auditory Countdown**: Four warning beeps provide an unmistakable ticking clock leading into commercial act breaks.

### 2. Structural Laws for TV Beat Sheets
- **The Act I Break**: Must not be the ultimate crisis; it is the moment the protagonist is pushed past the point of no return.
- **The Act II Break**: Must fundamentally transform the nature of the narrative (e.g., investigating a cold case shifts into surviving an active sniper assassination).
- **The Act III Break**: The protagonist's initial victory turns into a catastrophic new failure.

## IX. Sakamoto Yuji (*We Made a Beautiful Bouquet*): Dialogue-Driven Architecture

An exceptional Japanese case study in dialogue-driven character progression across five calendar years:
- **Asymmetric Narrative Weight**: The first year of romance occupies **42% of the script (73 scenes)**; the subsequent four years of decline collectively occupy the remaining 42%. *The sheer density of detail in the beginning embodies the memory of love.*
- **Diminishing Date Precision**: Year 1 is dated down to the exact day; Year 2 lists only dates; Year 3 lists only months; Year 4 lists seasons; Year 5 features zero dates. *The erosion of chronological precision reflects the decay of intimacy.*
- **Revisiting Locations**: Critical transitions occur in the exact same physical locations (the diner booth where they first confessed is where they finally decide to separate).
- **Budgeting Dialogue for Mirror Pairs**: The leads command 83% of the total dialogue. The highest-budgeted supporting characters are a young mirror couple who enter in the finale to reenact the leads' first meeting.

## X. Noh Hee-kyung (*The Most Beautiful Goodbye*): Terminal Family Drama

A masterclass in transforming terminal illness into dramatic reconciliation without melodrama:
- **Inverted Page Distribution**: The chapters describing terminal diagnosis are the shortest (26 pages); the chapters detailing the family preparing to say goodbye are the longest (44 pages); the death itself occupies a quiet 19 pages. *Focus on the preparation, not the demise.*
- **The Taboo Word**: Across the entire drama, the word *"Cancer"* is **never spoken aloud by the mother**. The withholding of the clinical diagnosis forces all conflict into behavioral subtext.
- **The Two Narrative Clocks**:
  - *The External Clock*: Moving into the new house in Ilsan before winter arrives.
  - *The Domestic Clock*: The traditional soybean paste pot (which spoils for the first time in thirty years).
- **Replacing Grief with Guilt**: The emotional engine of authentic family drama is not *"I have lost someone"*; it is **"Whose regret was I?"**
