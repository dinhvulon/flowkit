# Character & Conflict (sw-character-conflict)

> **Output Language:** English (or matching user query language). All terminology anchors to original craft terms (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)).

This skill unifies four foundational systems of character craft:
1. **Lajos Egri** (*The Art of Dramatic Writing*): Structural characterology (three dimensions, orchestration, unity of opposites, pivotal characters, four types of conflict, transitions).
2. **William Indick** (*Psychology for Screenwriters*): Deep psychological motivation (Freud, Erikson, Jung, Adler, Campbell, Murdock, Rollo May).
3. **Robert McKee, Neill Hicks, Lisa Cron, Blake Snyder**: Modern screenwriting pragmatics (characterization vs. true character, choice under pressure, self-concept, cognitive dissonance, the Third Rail, Save the Cat).
4. **Classic Dramatic Theory** (*Lu Jun, Tian Han, Wei Minglun*): Eight keys to living characters, arming the adversary, internal opposition, and eliminating artificial rescues.

## 1. What is Character?

### The Three-Dimensional Character (Lajos Egri)
- Every human being exists in three dimensions:
  1. **Physiology:** Sex, age, height, weight, posture, appearance, physical defects, heredity.
  2. **Sociology:** Class, occupation, education, family background, economic status, religion, race, politics.
  3. **Psychology:** Moral standards, personal premise and ambition, formative wounds, temperament, complexes, introversion/extroversion, phobias. *(Psychology is the inevitable product of physiology and sociology).*
- **Characters Create Plot, Not Vice Versa:** If you replace Hamlet with Romeo, Juliet never marries Paris; while Hamlet hesitates, Romeo acts. If Oedipus were not stubborn and proud, he would not kill the stranger at the crossroads or dig out his own eyes upon learning the truth.
- **Dialectical Necessity:** Show why it **must be this specific character** who is pushed to the edge, eliminating all easier alternatives.

### Characterization vs. True Character (Robert McKee)
- **Characterization:** The sum of all observable human qualities (physical appearance, speech cadence, quirks, profession, taste, dress).
- **True Character:** Revealed only through **choice under pressure**. The greater the pressure, the deeper the revelation of the character's core truth.
  - *Example:* Two drivers stop at a school bus crash—a janitor and a world-renowned neurosurgeon. Who rushes into the flames? Who calls for help? Who saves which child first? Their immediate actions reveal true character.
- **Dimensions = Contradictions:** Dimensions are not a laundry list of quirky hobbies. A dimension is an active inner contradiction (e.g., a ruthless, ambitious executive plagued by deep moral guilt; a charming, gentle thief).
- **The Solar System Principle:**
  - The protagonist is the **Sun**.
  - Supporting characters are **Planets** whose orbits exist to draw out different dimensions of the protagonist.
  - Minor characters are **Moons/Satellites** who possess a single memorable trait and vanish.
  - The protagonist **must be the most multi-dimensional character in the story**. If a villain or sidekick has more dimensions than the lead, the center of gravity collapses.

### Character Invocation & Self-Concept (Neill Hicks)
- **The Writer as Discoverer:** Do not rigidly construct characters from spreadsheets; listen to them speak. If you are not surprised by what your character says or does, you are micromanaging them.
- **The Minimal Action Rule:** A character will always take the least disruptive, most conservative action possible to satisfy their desire without exposing their hidden psychological vulnerability. The writer’s job is to continually close off easy escapes, forcing dramatic decisions.
- **Self-Concept & Cognitive Dissonance:**
  - **Self-Concept:** How the character views themselves versus who they actually are.
  - **Cognitive Dissonance:** The painful friction when external circumstances shatter their self-concept, forcing an internal reckoning.

### Desire vs. Misbelief (Lisa Cron)
- Every protagonist enters the story with an existing survival strategy rooted in a formative **Misbelief** (learned in the Origin Scene). The plot's sole purpose is to systematically challenge and dismantle this misbelief until the protagonist breaks through to clarity.

### Eight Keys to Living Characters (Lu Jun)
1. **Conceive from Character First:** Plots and characters must mature simultaneously; never invent arbitrary plot mechanics and force characters into them like puppets.
2. **Distinctive Behavioral Action:** Engels: *"Character is revealed not only by what a person does, but by how they do it."* Two men seeking revenge behave entirely differently based on character (one uses cold calculation; the other strikes with brutal directness).
3. **Reject Simplistic Caricatures:** Cruelty often masks weakness; tenderness can conceal unyielding steel. Never flatten opponents into mindless monsters.
4. **Dynamic Evolution:** A character who merely repeats two or three fixed traits is an equation, not a human being. Characters must evolve through progressive choices.
5. **Sensory Behavioral Details:** Concrete, specific habits that convey an entire personal history in a single second.
6. **Purposeful Entrance:** The moment and method of a character’s first entrance must directly serve the dramatic conflict.
7. **Distinctive Verbal Voice:** Every character must speak with unique syntax, vocabulary, rhythm, and moral assumptions.
8. **Authentic Typicality:** The character feels unmistakably familiar, embodying broad human truths, yet remains utterly unique.

## 2. The Protagonist

- **Egri’s Pivotal Character:**
  - The engine of dramatic momentum. Must possess an urgent, burning desire that is more important to them than life itself.
  - Must be placed in a critical emergency, displaying relentless aggression and tenacity.
  - *Pivotal characters have less room for broad emotional growth because their fundamental decision is locked early; their journey is about pushing that decision to its ultimate conclusion.*
- **McKee’s Protagonist:**
  - Must possess willpower, a conscious desire, and an unconscious counter-desire.
  - Must have at least one viable chance to achieve their goal, pursuing it to the ultimate boundary of their world and genre.
  - **Empathy over Sympathy:** The audience must identify with the protagonist's human struggle (*"They are like me"*); they do not need to find them polite or saintly.
- **Blake Snyder’s Principles:**
  - The hero must have the longest, most arduous emotional arc to travel.
  - **Save the Cat:** Give the audience an early reason to root for them through an act of generosity, humor, or vulnerability. If the hero is flawed, make the antagonist significantly more depraved.
- **Psychological Arc (Indick & Rollo May):**
  - The protagonist represents the **Ego** navigating between the primitive drives of the **Id** (the Antagonist) and the moral demands of the **Superego** (the Mentor).
  - **The Leap of Faith:** The moment of transformation cannot be a breezy 10-second agreement; it must be an earned, agonizing internal death of the old self followed by an active behavioral commitment.

## 3. The Antagonist / Opposing Force

- **Egri’s Principle:** The antagonist must be every bit as strong, intelligent, and unyielding as the protagonist.
- **McKee’s Principle of Antagonism:**
  - *"The protagonist and their story can only be as intellectually fascinating and emotionally compelling as the forces of antagonism make them."*
  - The forces of antagonism are the sum total of all resistance (internal doubts, interpersonal friction, societal oppression, environmental brutality).
  - At the Inciting Incident, the protagonist must be the clear underdog.
- **The Four Levels of Negative Progression:**
  1. **Positive Value:** Justice, Love, Freedom.
  2. **Contrary Value (The Opposite):** Injustice, Indifference, Constraint.
  3. **Contradictory Value (The Direct Negation):** Corruption, Hate, Tyranny.
  4. **The Negation of the Negation (The Deeper Corruption):** Self-righteous cruelty masquerading as justice; possessive destruction masquerading as love; total slavery masquerading as ultimate freedom.
- **Hicks’ Antagonist Mechanics:**
  - The antagonist must possess vastly superior resources, power, and leverage.
  - **The antagonist's story is already well underway**; the protagonist intrudes upon the antagonist's world.
- **Psychology of the Villain (Indick):**
  - The villain is the unfettered **Id** operating without social shame or moral restraint.
  - **Inferiority & Overcompensation (Adler):** Behind every towering tyrant lies a profound, hidden wound or sense of inadequacy that drives their manic pursuit of control.
  - **Poetic Justice:** Retribution must fit the crime. The villain must be defeated by the protagonist's own hands, often undone by the very weapon or strategy they relied upon.
- **Classic Dramaturgy for Adversaries (Lu Jun):**
  - **A 3-to-2 nail-biter is infinitely more thrilling than a 9-to-0 blowout.**
  - **Give the Opponent a Knife:** Strip the protagonist of easy advantages while arming the adversary with superior legal, social, physical, or moral leverage.
  - **The Strongest Opponent is Oneself:** External combat becomes truly tragic and resonant only when it mirrors the hero's internal civil war.
  - **No Deus Ex Machina (Prohibiting "Divine Clouds"):** Never resolve a crisis through sudden bureaucratic decrees, accidental rescues, or coincidental inheritances. If the adversary's overwhelming power reflects the harsh truth of the world, write a tragedy.

## 4. Character Orchestration & Unity of Opposites (Egri)

- **Orchestration (The Ensemble Symphony):**
  - If everyone is an aggressive bully, the cast sounds like a symphony made entirely of brass drums.
  - Orchestrate contrasting temperaments, tempos, vocabularies, and worldviews (the Pragmatist vs. the Zealot; the Stoic vs. the Volatile).
  - Clear orchestration guarantees that dialogue will naturally diverge without artificial effort.
- **The Unity of Opposites:**
  - A genuine unity of opposites **cannot be compromised**.
  - The opposing forces are irrevocably bound together by family blood, love, legal contract, shared peril, or an inescapable secret.
  - The bond can only be broken when one side's dominant characteristic is decisively shattered.
  - *Without unbreakable unity, characters would simply walk away from each other, killing the drama.*

## 5. Movement and Types of Conflict (Egri)

- Every physical action is the direct product of an underlying cause.
- **The Four Types of Conflict:**
  1. **Static Conflict:** Characters share identical worldviews or lack firm conviction; polite, witty banter that circles endlessly without driving decisions.
  2. **Jumping Conflict:** Characters make sudden, unearned behavioral leaps that violate their established psychology (e.g., an unrepentant criminal suddenly repents after a single gentle word).
  3. **Escalating (Rising) Conflict:** The organic result of a clear premise, 3D characters, and an unbreakable unity of opposites. Every attack triggers an even more ferocious counter-attack.
  4. **Foreshadowed Conflict:** The simmering, electric tension established when two uncompromising forces are placed in an unavoidable pressure chamber, promising an inevitable explosion.
- **The Law of Transitions:**
  - *"Nature never jumps."*
  - An emotional escalation must transition step-by-step: Affection → Disappointment → Annoyance → Irritation → Open Anger → Aggression → Threats → Premeditation → Violence.
  - Skipping steps without behavioral bridges turns drama into unearned **melodrama**.
- **Point of Attack:** Launch the story at the exact moment a major crisis forces a critical decision. Enter the battle when the fuse is already lit, not when the dynamite is being manufactured.

## 6. The Psychological Toolbox (William Indick)

- **Freudian Architecture:**
  - **Id:** Primitive desire, passion, chaos (the Antagonist).
  - **Ego:** Conscious reality, tactical survival, logic (the Protagonist).
  - **Superego:** Conscience, societal ideals, moral law (the Mentor).
- **Defense Mechanisms (Unconscious Shields):**
  - *Repression:* Burying trauma until a sensory trigger causes an explosion.
  - *Denial:* Refusing to accept reality until forced by undeniable crisis.
  - *Reaction Formation:* Expressing the exact opposite of what is felt (overcompensating puritanism masking illicit lust).
  - *Projection:* Accusing others of the exact flaws the character cannot face in themselves.
  - *Sublimation:* Channeling destructive rage or forbidden longing into obsessive professional work.
- **Jungian Archetypes:**
  - **Persona:** The polished social mask presented to the public.
  - **Shadow:** The repressed dark impulses, cowardice, or savage traits the hero refuses to acknowledge. The story climax requires the hero to face and integrate their shadow.
  - **Anima/Animus:** The idealized inner feminine/masculine counterpart projected onto the romantic interest.
  - **Shapeshifter:** The ally whose loyalty remains tantalizingly ambiguous.

## 7. Character Transformation & Arc

- **The Arc is Structural:** Changing a character's core choices alters the plot; altering plot events demands a re-examination of character psychology.
- **The Five Steps of McKee's Character Arc:**
  1. Establish characterization (the outer mask).
  2. Reveal true character through an initial choice under moderate pressure.
  3. Challenge the character's inner core through escalating external friction.
  4. Force a brutal choice between two impossible alternatives under maximum pressure.
  5. The character executes an irreversible action, cementing their new humanity.
- **The Boundaries of the Vacillating Character:**
  - A character who wavers or doubts is essential for dramatic tension, but their wavering must have a ceiling and a floor.
  - If they never waver, there is no suspense; if they waver completely without conviction, they become pathetic and forfeit audience empathy.

## 8. Diagnostic Checklist for Characters & Conflict

1. Can you state the protagonist's central conflict and driving desire in two clear sentences?
2. Are all three dimensions (Physiological, Sociological, Psychological) fully understood for all major characters?
3. Does the protagonist possess an urgent desire that is more important than survival?
4. Is there an unbreakable **Unity of Opposites** binding protagonist and adversary?
5. Is the antagonist significantly stronger, wealthier, or more ruthless than the protagonist at the start?
6. Does the conflict escalate step-by-step, or does it make unearned "jumps"?
7. Is the Point of Attack placed at a moment of acute decision, rather than leisurely backstory?
8. Does every major character possess a distinctive verbal rhythm, syntax, and behavioral signature?
9. Is the climax free of artificial rescues (deus ex machina)? Does the protagonist win or lose through their own deliberate agency?
10. Is the protagonist measurably different—wiser, more integrated, or tragically broken—at the end compared to the beginning?

## 9. End-to-End Workflow

1. **Deduce Character from Premise:** Identify the necessary character traits required to prove the core premise.
2. **Draft the 3D Profiles:** Outline physiological, sociological, and psychological baselines. Identify the hidden Misbelief and the Origin Scene.
3. **Establish Orchestration & Solar System:** Check that supporting characters represent distinct worldviews and highlight different facets of the protagonist.
4. **Lock the Unity of Opposites:** Ensure the hero and adversary cannot simply walk away.
5. **Arm the Adversary:** Give the opponent superior weapons, status, or leverage; identify their underlying wound/inferiority.
6. **Map the Point of Attack:** Launch the narrative at the threshold of a critical, irreversible choice.
7. **Trace the Ten-Step Escalation Ladder:** Outline each progressive conflict beat, ensuring smooth behavioral transitions without melodramatic jumps.
8. **Integrate the Shadow:** Force the hero to confront their repressed flaws during the Dark Night of the Soul.
9. **Execute the Climax Decision:** Structure the ultimate choice as a genuine dilemma between two irreconcilable values.
10. **The Final Audit:** Verify that true character is revealed through action, not dialogue, and that the protagonist earns their transformation.
