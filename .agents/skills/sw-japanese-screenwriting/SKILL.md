# Japanese Screenwriting Methods & Craft

> **Output Language = Prompt Language**; terminology is strictly anchored to industry-standard screenwriting concepts (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)). Never invent non-standard translations.

The practical craft, narrative philosophies, and development workflows of Japanese screenwriting. Synthesized from *From Zero to Screenwriter: 10 Master Japanese Directors & Writers on Script Craft* (Takahiro Tomari ed.) and Haruhiko Arai (*Hollywood Masterclasses: International Edition*). 

### The Japanese Dichotomy
- **The Classical Structuralists** (Kosawa Ryota, Uchida Kenji, Kakei Masaya): Recognize the universal utility of the 3-act paradigm and classical *Kishōtenketsu* (起承転結), but investigate *why* great stories naturally form these shapes rather than mechanically following Hollywood beat sheets.
- **The Organic Materialists** (Miki Satoshi, Sono Sion, Yokohama Satoko, Takahashi Izumi, Fukuda Yuichi): Abandon pre-planned outlines. Themes are discovered late; plots are generated from accumulated character fragments, odd observations, and human flaws.
- **The Collaborative Pragmatists** (Isao Yukisada): View the screenplay as a flexible "coordinate axis" and a catalyst for collaborative discussion rather than an immutable literary monument.

*(For the specific minimalist, elliptical family drama style of Ozu Yasujiro and Noda Kogo, see `ozu-screenplay-style`. For South Korean and French methodologies, see `sw-korean-french-screenwriting`.)*

Extended case studies, pitch documents (企画書), and scene outlines are located in [reference.md](reference.md).

## I. Two Opposing Schools: Structure-First vs. Fragment-First

| Parameter | The Structure-First School | The Fragment-First School |
|---|---|---|
| **Representative Writers** | Kosawa Ryota, Uchida Kenji, Kakei Masaya | Miki Satoshi, Sono Sion, Yokohama Satoko, Fukuda Yuichi, Takahashi Izumi |
| **Genesis of Story** | Premise rules, high-concept "If + Moreover", predetermined ending. | The "Toy Box" notebook, actor personalities, personal shame, character diaries. |
| **Discovery of Theme** | Defined prior to writing or dictated by the structural setup. | **Emerges retrospectively** at 70–80% completion; never pre-imposed. |
| **Plot Outlines** | Mandatory. Grids, timelines, multi-episode A4 master charts. | Refused or minimal (*"Outlining suffocates spontaneous explosive energy"*). |
| **Inherent Vulnerability** | Elegant structural mechanics can feel emotionally sterile. | Connecting disjointed episodic fragments can feel like creative torture. |

> **Consensus Across Both Schools**: Complete the draft. As Miki Satoshi insists: *"Turn the toy box upside down and write the first draft!"* Show your work to readers: finding someone who says *"This is boring, and here is why"* is infinitely more valuable than receiving polite praise.

## II. Structure, Pace & Rhythm

### 1. Kishōtenketsu (起承転結) as Accelerated 3-Act Structure
Kosawa Ryota (*Legal High, The Confidence Man JP*) translates Eastern dramatic structure into modern pacing:
- **Ki (起 - Setup)**: Rapidly establish character relationships and baseline dynamics.
- **Shō (承 - Complication / Development)**: The engine of the script. The richer and more varied the complications in *Shō*, the higher the quality of the narrative. Multiple comedic or dramatic twists occur here.
- **Ten & Ketsu (転 & 結 - Climax & Resolution)**: Fused into an explosive finale.

### 2. Plot Points as Moments of Active Commitment
Uchida Kenji (*A Stranger of Mine, Key of Life*): A plot point is not a random external catastrophe; **it is the exact second the protagonist commits to a definitive action**.
- *E.T.*: The moment the boy decides to hide the alien in his bedroom.
- *Die Hard*: The moment John McClane picks up the submachine gun.
Think in four distinct 30-minute movements.

### 3. Pre-Determining the Destination
Begin with the ending. As Uchida observes, arriving at the airport without knowing your destination creates aimless drifting. Sono Sion (*Love Exposure*) began with two precise images (preaching to his sister on the beach, and the sister breaking him out of the psychiatric asylum) and reverse-engineered the preceding four hours.

### 4. Velocity Through Narrative Leaping (Kosawa Ryota)
- **Dynamic Cross-Cutting**: Intercutting multiple simultaneous narrative threads creates relentless forward momentum.
- **The "Leap" Principle**: Cut scenes that lack narrative jumps. A scene must leap 2–3 levels forward.
- **Dialogue Contradiction**: Let consecutive lines of dialogue negate each other. Let consecutive scenes showcase opposing emotional polarities.
- **Rhythmic Volatility**: Inspired by *Lost* and *Neon Genesis Evangelion*, keep the audience off-balance by pivoting narrative trajectories unexpectedly.

### 5. Terminating Exposition Instantly (Miki Satoshi)
End contextual explanations as fast as possible. In *Instant Swamp*, Miki delivers the entire backstory across 108 rapid cuts in 2.5 minutes using voiceover and physical gags, freeing the remaining 90 minutes for pure comedic narrative.

### 6. The 10-Minute TV Rule
In episodic television, a decisive disruption must strike within the first 10 minutes (before the first commercial break) so the audience understands the central question of the week.

### 7. Stories Where Nothing Happens (Anti-Growth)
Reject the Western mandate that characters must undergo radical moral transformation. Miki Satoshi: *"Does a human being really have to improve? A fool who remains a fool from beginning to end is deeply charming and true to life."* Characters in *Adrift in Tokyo* or *Turtles Swim Faster Than Expected* experience seasonal drift rather than monumental redemption.

### 8. Repetition and Deviation in Daily Life (Isao Yukisada)
Echoing Ozu: capture the quiet poignancy of daily routines, then observe the microscopic deviations—taking a different path at the cherry blossom crossroads, or lingering over a cup of tea because of another person's presence.

## III. Material, Concepts & The Retrospective Theme

### 1. The Daily Observation Habit
- Record at least one bizarre, funny, or provocative real-world observation every day. Reviewing 180 entries after six months reveals your subconscious obsessions.
- **The "Toy Box" Inversion**: Empty all accumulated notes onto the floor, cluster related fragments, and search for the hidden connecting thread.

### 2. The Concept Collision: "If" + "Moreover" (Kakei Masaya)
A compelling concept is the collision of two radically distant realities:
$$\textbf{Concept} = \textbf{Premise (If)} + \textbf{Unrelated Complication (Moreover)}$$
- *"A beautiful woman crawls out of a canned food container"* + *"Moreover, she encounters an unimaginative young theater clerk."*
- *"A synchronized swimmer"* + *"Moreover, they are all high school boys (*Waterboys*)."*
- *Rule*: *"Moreover"* validates coincidence and creates fresh comedic collisions; *"Therefore"* merely creates predictable, linear plotting.

### 3. Non-Linear Timelines Cost Zero Yen (Uchida Kenji)
Rearranging chronological time is the greatest weapon for independent, low-budget cinema. You can shoot a mundane conversation in a single room, but rearranging the sequence of events across multiple perspectives transforms ordinary interactions into a thrilling puzzle box.

### 4. "Write What You Are Ashamed Of" (Haruhiko Arai)
Authentic personal experience should never be copied literally; it must be mined for emotional vulnerability:
> **"What you must do as a writer is expose the things that make you deeply ashamed to the audience."**

Audiences are not moved by heroic self-portraits; they connect with cowardly impulses, petty vanities, and unreciprocated longing.

### 5. The Retrospective Theme
Do not pre-determine your moral theme before typing Page 1. Write forward organically through character collisions. Around Episode 7 or 8, step back and ask: *"What are these people actually searching for?"*
*Example*: Miki Satoshi realized deep into writing that all his characters were waiting for something—which led to setting the climax in Rumoi, a coastal town historically built on waiting for the herring catch. **Once the theme reveals itself retrospectively, go back and ruthlessly prune away scenes that do not harmonize with it.**

## IV. Character Architecture: Humanity Over Tropes

### 1. Character Bios & Private Diaries (Uchida Kenji)
Before scripting, draft the protagonist's private diary for the three days leading up to the story. If a character acts cowardly on page 30, explore how their secret diary entries justify their fear. The audience only sees the tip of the iceberg; the writer must build the submerged mass.

### 2. Character = Actor + Distinct Flaw (Fukuda Yuichi)
When casting or writing for specific actors:
- Focus on the actor's unique physical rhythm and charisma.
- **Anchor the character in an immediate behavioral flaw** (80% brilliance, 20% ridiculous incompetence).
- Spider-Man becomes hilarious the moment he cannot shoot web because a girl looked at him; Batman is less fun because he lacks pathetic petty flaws. Audiences love characters who lie frantically to protect their small egos.

### 3. "Jinpin" (人品 - Human Texture) Over Mechanical Archetypes (Takahashi Izumi)
Instead of building a cardboard "academic" whose room is filled with leather-bound treatises, Takahashi gives the professor two well-thumbed copies of *Doraemon* on his bedside table. Observe the messy contradictions of living human beings.

### 4. The Power of the Triad (Miki Satoshi)
Two characters in conflict produce a stalemate of opposing arguments. Adding a **third character** destabilizes the equation: when alliances shift between three people, values, power balances, and narrative trajectory pivot instantly.

### 5. The Outside Witness (Kosawa Ryota)
Introduce an ordinary outsider into a bizarre subculture (e.g., an inexperienced, naive recruit assigned to elite foreign intelligence). The outsider's bewilderment serves as the audience's surrogate, revealing the insanity of the institution.

## V. Dialogue Craft

### 1. Dialogue Born of Internal Monologue (Omiya Ellie)
Genuine dialogue does not come from observing external social chatter; it comes from the relentless, month-long conversations a writer has with their own conscience. Express the unedited interior confessions of the heart.

### 2. Preserving Ambiguity & Sonic Texture (Yokohama Satoko)
- Avoid spelling out psychological motivations. Keep dialogue ambiguous so different audience members interpret lines differently.
- **Prioritize the Sonic Rhythm**: How words sound aloud matters more than semantic precision.
- **Avoid Emotional Adjectives**: Depict physical actions rather than having characters declare *"I am heartbroken."*

### 3. Writing Dialogue First to Generate Plot (Fukuda Yuichi)
Start typing raw dialogue exchanges between two contrasting characters before planning the narrative beats. Let the conversation wander into strange, hilarious corners. **"I warmly welcome running off-track."**

### 4. Delegating the Tsukkomi Role to the Audience
In traditional Japanese comedy, a joke requires a *Boke* (funny man / idiot) and a *Tsukkomi* (straight man / corrector). Fukuda's technique: **Remove the Tsukkomi from the scene entirely**. Let two or three absolute idiots bounce absurd statements off each other without anyone correcting them. The audience in the theater naturally steps into the role of the Tsukkomi, thinking: *"Somebody stop these idiots!"*

## VI. Originals vs. Adaptations: Constraints as Creative Engines

### 1. Originality Born from External Constraints (Isao Yukisada)
Total freedom produces paralyzing writer's block:
> **"Establish the strict constraints that fit your project, and creative breakthroughs will happen automatically. Being told 'Write whatever you want' is the most dangerous trap in cinema."**

- Limited budgets, restricted locations, or specific commercial briefs (e.g., a music video brief transformed into a story about burning textbooks) become powerful creative weapons.
- As Uchida Kenji notes: *"Cooking is much easier when you are only permitted to use cabbage and eggs."*

### 2. The Value of the "Moderately Imperfect" Script
A brilliant script should not be an airtight, suffocating literary monolith. It should leave imaginative breathing room for the director, cinematographer, and actors to say: *"I know how to bring this alive."* A script exists to spark generative creative arguments on set.

## VII. Master Diagnostic Checklist

### Concept & Premise
- [ ] Is your concept a collision of two distant ideas using *"Moreover"* rather than predictable linear logic (*"Therefore"*)?
- [ ] Does your title provoke an immediate question (*"What on earth happens in this film?"*)?
- [ ] Can you summarize the human core of the narrative in 1–2 plain sentences?

### Structure & Pacing
- [ ] Does a decisive disruption occur within the first 10 minutes?
- [ ] Does the narrative accelerate through contrasting scenes and contradictory dialogue?
- [ ] Have you eliminated lingering exposition within the opening 3 minutes?
- [ ] Are you honoring the *Shō* (complication) phase by allowing multiple unexpected turns?

### Character & Humanity
- [ ] Have you identified the lead character's primary behavioral flaw (their 20% ridiculousness)?
- [ ] Does the character possess unique human texture (*Jinpin*) rather than generic occupational tropes?
- [ ] Have you deployed a triad to prevent ideological stalemates?
- [ ] Did you write the character's private diary for the days preceding the story?

### Dialogue & Tone
- [ ] Have you removed emotional self-diagnosis (*"I feel depressed"*) in favor of concrete physical behavior?
- [ ] Does your comedic rhythm delegate the *Tsukkomi* to the audience rather than over-explaining the punchlines?
- [ ] Have you mined authentic personal vulnerability and exposed what makes you ashamed?
- [ ] Have you pruned all moralizing preachiness once the central theme emerged?

## VIII. Step-by-Step Original Japanese Screenwriting Workflow

```
[1. The Toy Box Inversion] ➔ [2. Concept Collision ("If + Moreover")] ➔ [3. The Destination]
        ➔ [4. Character Diaries & Flaws] ➔ [5. The 4-Movement Outline]
        ➔ [6. Rapid Dialogue Drafting] ➔ [7. Retrospective Theme Audit] ➔ [8. The Kikakusho (企画書)]
```

1. **Step 1: The Toy Box Inversion**: Review months of raw notes and doodles. Cluster the recurring obsessions.
2. **Step 2: Concept Collision**: Fuse two unrelated elements into a provocative premise.
3. **Step 3: Define the Destination**: Anchor the final scene or emotional image before mapping the journey.
4. **Step 4: Character Architecture**: Establish the flaw, the human quirks, and write 3 days of private diary entries.
5. **Step 5: The Four Movements**: Map the story across four 30-minute movements (or 10-minute television blocks).
6. **Step 6: Rapid Dialogue Drafting**: Draft scenes with forward momentum, allowing characters to surprise you.
7. **Step 7: Retrospective Theme Audit**: At the 75% mark, identify the unstated theme and prune away redundant scenes.
8. **Step 8: The Kikakusho (企画書)**: Draft the television pitch document:
   - High-Concept Logline & Commercial Comps.
   - Character Dossiers (catchphrases, perspective roles, complementary flaws).
   - Episode 1 & 2 Detailed Outlines + Season Arc Titles.
   - Director's Statement: *"Why an original screenplay must defeat adaptations through sheer accessibility."*
