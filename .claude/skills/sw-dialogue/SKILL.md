# Dialogue Craft & Subtext (sw-dialogue)

> **Output Language:** English (or matching user query language). All terminology anchors to original craft terms (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)).

The core premise of dialogue craft is singular: **Dialogue is action** (Robert McKee: *dia* + *legein* = action through words). Beneath every utterance lies conscious desire, underlying intention, and a tactical verbal strategy. *"Flaws in dialogue are invariably flaws in character or story design."* If dialogue cannot be fixed on the page, the writer must look back at the underlying scene objectives and dramatic conflict.

---

## 1. What is Dialogue?

- **The Comprehensive Definition:** Anything spoken by any character to anyone—to another person, to oneself (*all internal monologue is dialogue*), or directly to the audience (voiceover, soliloquy, breaking the fourth wall).
  - Characters never speak without an underlying desire: every line executes an intention, pursues an objective, and applies a verbal tactic.
- **Dramatic Dialogue vs. Narrative Dialogue:**
  - *Dramatic Dialogue:* Inside the scene, saturated with immediate conflict, each line executes a behavioral action demanding a reaction.
  - *Narrative Dialogue:* Outside the scene, speaking across the fourth wall to orient the audience.
- **Three Primary Functions:**
  1. Exposition (dispensing story facts).
  2. Characterization (establishing outer personality and social mask).
  3. Action (forcing value changes through behavioral conflict).
- **The Three Concentric Spheres of Dialogue Content:**
  1. **The Said:** The conscious, audible words, syntax, vocabulary, and tone.
  2. **The Unsaid (Subtext):** The thoughts, intentions, and feelings the character consciously hides. Audiences instinctively look past spoken words to decipher subtext.
  3. **The Unsayable:** The unconscious, repressed desires and psychological drives that the character cannot admit even to themselves, revealed only under severe crisis.
  - *As language, dialogue reveals characterization. As action, dialogue reveals true character.*
- **Action vs. Physical Behavior:** Eating ice cream is rarely about hunger—it is about soothing grief, defying a doctor, or rewarding oneself. Unconscious motivation is the true action. Silence and pauses are active behavioral choices.
- **The Screenwriter's Three Questions for Every Line:**
  1. What does the character want in this room right now?
  2. What tactical action are they employing to get it?
  3. What words would they choose to execute that action?
  - Label beats with active gerund phrases (*"Pleading for mercy" / "Stonewalling with icy sarcasm"*).
- **Medium Differences:**
  - *Theatre:* Auditory-first; heightened, poetic language is celebrated.
  - *Cinema:* Visual-first; the camera mercilessly magnifies insincerity and excessive talk. Naturalistic, performative, leaves room for actors.
  - *Television:* Intimate, conversational, close-up face-to-face conflict.
  - *General Law:* The greater the external physical conflict, the less dialogue is needed; the more intimate the psychological conflict, the more dialogue is required.
- **Richard Walter:** Most producers read only the dialogue, giving you every reason to make it crackle. Yet the greatest dialogue line is often **no dialogue at all**—a loaded gaze, a removed ring, or a deep breath.

## 2. Exposition: Dramatized, Weaponized, and Kept Secret

- Provide only the bare minimum information needed to understand the immediate scene; never dispense backstory prematurely.
- **Dramatize, Don't Lecture:**
  - *Bad:* "Charlie, we've known each other for twenty years since college, how are you this morning?" (Dead exposition).
  - *Good:* "You haven't changed one bit from the immature slob you were twenty years ago in the dorms—smell your coat." (Audience learns the 20-year history through an active insult).
- **Weaponize Exposition:**
  - Information should be wielded as an offensive blade or defensive shield by characters to achieve their scene objectives.
  - *Example:* Darth Vader revealing *"I am your father"* at the climax of a lightsaber duel to shatter Luke's psychic defenses.
- **The Ultimate Exposition is a Secret:**
  - Backstory is not a comprehensive biographical timeline; it is a hidden past truth withheld until an All Is Lost moment where revealing it forces a crisis.
- **Expository Sins to Prohibit:**
  - Opening scrolling text or voiceover that recaps lore that could be dramatized.
  - Characters reciting facts to each other that both already know (*"As you know, Bob, my brother died five years ago..."*).
  - Richard Walter's Principle 28: **Never allow Character A to tell Character B information that the audience already knows.**

## 3. Six Tasks and Four Flaws of Dialogue (Robert McKee)

### The Six Tasks of Dialogue
1. Execute an inner action with every line.
2. Advance action/reaction beats toward a scene turning point.
3. Convey story facts and character worldview naturally.
4. Individualize each character with a unique vocal signature.
5. Capture and command the audience's attention through progressive rhythm.
6. Strike the reader emotionally while remaining organically true to the character.

### 1. Flaws of Credibility
- Casual conversation burns time; cinematic dialogue focuses meaning.
- **Empty Words:** Repeating mundane pleasantries that convey zero subtext.
- **Over-Perception:** Giving characters a Freudian vocabulary where they psychoanalyze themselves like Socrates.
- **Confusing Excuses with Motives:** Conscious dialogue gives the character's surface excuse (*"I'm doing this for your own good"*); true motive is the unconscious drive.
- **Melodrama is Caused by Insufficient Motivation, Not Passion:** If the stakes and motivations are deeply earned, an explosive scream feels natural; if motivation is shallow, even a raised voice feels melodramatic.

### 2. Flaws of Language
- **Clichés:** The first phrasing that comes to mind is almost always an unoriginal trope. Brainstorm alternative phrasings and discard the obvious choices.
- **Character-Neutral Speech:** Generic dialogue that sounds like any writer from nowhere. Distinct cultures and regions produce distinct idioms:
  - Generic: *"Oh my god."*
  - Alabama: *"Sweet, bleeding Jesus."*
  - Detroit: *"Lord have mercy."*
  - Brooklyn: *"Well, damn me to hell."*
- **Overwritten / Self-Indulgent Dialogue:** Lines that scream *"Look how clever the writer is!"* If a line calls attention to itself rather than the character, cut it.
- **Barren / Academic Language:**
  - Choose the concrete over the abstract.
  - Choose short, visceral Anglo-Saxon words over sprawling multi-syllabic Latinate words (*"He lied"* instead of *"His statement represents an intentional falsification of reality"*).
  - High emotion demands shorter words and shorter sentences.
  - Eliminate verbal clutter: *"Well"*, *"Look"*, *"Listen"*, *"Actually"*, *"You know"*.

### 3. Flaws of Content
- **On-the-Nose Dialogue (Writing the Subtext into the Text):**
  - Characters stating their exact innermost feelings directly: *"I am terrified you don't love me anymore."*
  - On-the-nose dialogue strangles an actor's performance because it leaves nothing for them to play underneath.
  - *Real human beings rarely say what they mean when emotional stakes are at their highest.*
- **The Inner Monologue Trap:**
  - Writing endless, uninterrupted monologues where a character preaches the film's theme. Break long speeches with physical interruptions or reactions from bystanders.
- **Converting Rigid Two-Handers (Duologues) into Trialogues:**
  - Two characters arguing back and forth in an empty room quickly feels repetitive and flat.
  - **The Trialogue Technique:** Insert a third element as the battleground or emotional lightning rod (a shared physical object, a bystander, a pet, a waiter, or an absent third party). The characters fight through their reactions to the third element.

### 4. Flaws of Design
- **Repetitive Beats:** Characters repeating the same tactical beat (pleading, then pleading again with different words, then pleading a third time). Each beat must escalate to a new tactic.
- **The Suspensive Sentence (Delayed Punchline):**
  - Place the operational, load-bearing word at the very end of the sentence:
    - Weak: *"Why did you point that gun at me with that menacing look?"*
    - Strong: *"Why did you give me that... gun?"*
  - In comedy and dramatic confrontations, saving the operative word for the final syllable delivers maximum impact.
- **Divergent Scenes:** Where surface dialogue and hidden subtext become completely disconnected, confusing the audience instead of creating delicious irony.

## 4. Rhythm, Syntax, Silence, and the Rule of perpetual Argument

- **Sensory Rhetoric:** Use visceral metaphors drawn from the character's specific trade or physical environment.
- **Economy:** Screenwriters are not paid by the word. Cut sentences in half, then cut them again.
- **The Power of Pauses and Silence:**
  - Pauses must be earned through unbearable dramatic tension, not tossed around casually.
  - A character turning away and taking a slow, trembling breath often delivers a more devastating reversal than a monologue.
- **Richard Walter’s Principle 27: Perpetual Argument:**
  - In ordinary life, agreement and harmony are healthy; in drama, agreement is fatal.
  - Let every line of dialogue challenge, question, deflect, or push back against the preceding line.
  - Avoid polite consensus: treat every exchange as an ideological or tactical sparring match.
- **Walter's Forbidden Screenwriting Crutches:**
  - Eliminate *"I think"*, *"Listen to me"*, *"You know what I mean"*.
  - Prohibit excessive exclamation points and ellipses.
  - Avoid parenthetical acting instructions (e.g., `(angrily)`, `(tearfully)`); if the dialogue line doesn't convey the emotion, rewriting the line is the only cure.

## 5. Crafting the Character's Specific Verbal Voice

- **Vocabulary Defines the World:**
  - A mechanic perceives reality through structural wear, torque, and combustion; an investment banker perceives reality through leverage, margins, and liquidation.
  - Nouns and verbs reveal the character's scope of knowledge and worldview.
  - Modifiers, voice cadence, and rhythm reveal psychological vulnerability.
- **Blake Snyder’s Cover-the-Names Test:**
  - Cover the character names on the left margin of your script.
  - If you cannot instantly identify which character is speaking from their vocabulary, sentence length, and syntax alone, the voices are muddy and homogenously written like the screenwriter.
- **The Rule of Specific Idioms:**
  - Avoid cheap phonetic spellings of dialects. Convey cultural and regional flavor through sentence architecture, word order, and distinct cultural metaphors rather than dropped apostrophes.

## 6. The Aesthetic Dimensions of Dialogue (Lu Jun)

- **Poetic Elegance vs. Vernacular Truth:**
  - *Poetic Elegance:* Lyrical, metaphorical, concise, and charged with profound emotional resonance.
  - *Vernacular Truth (Natural Authenticity):* Grounded in real street slang, colorful vernacular, and colloquial directness.
  - Great dramatic dialogue marries vernacular authenticity with subtle, poetic subtext: simple on the surface, infinitely deep underneath.
- **The Three Golden Demands of Dialogue:**
  1. **Action-Driven:** Every line moves the scene's power balance.
  2. **Character-Specific:** Impossible for any other character in the story to speak that sentence.
  3. **Subtext-Rich:** Words that mean far more than their literal definitions.

## 7. Comedy Dialogue & Screwball Cross-Talk

- **The Engine of Comedy:**
  - Comedic characters are driven by blind, rigid obsessions and emotional tunnel vision.
  - They treat absurd, trivial problems with deadly life-or-death seriousness.
- **Four Comedic Mechanics:**
  1. *Absolute Clarity:* Jokes fail when the setup is confusing or cluttered with excess words.
  2. *Exaggeration / Understatement:* Catastrophizing a minor inconvenience or deadpanning through an apocalypse.
  3. *Comic Timing:* Set up the premise, deliver the twist, and anchor the punchline on the final syllable—never speak over the punchline.
  4. *Incongruity:* Sophisticated adults behaving like petulant toddlers, or hardened gangsters debating domestic etiquette.
- **Screwball Cross-Talk:**
  - Rapid-fire verbal sparring where neither party cedes an inch.
  - The female protagonist is razor-sharp, fiercely independent, and intellectually superior.
  - Sexual tension is sublimated into competitive wit, rapid interruptions, and rhythmic banter.

## 8. Step-by-Step Dialogue Beat Analysis (McKee)

1. **Expose the Core Desires:** Detail each character's conscious scene objective, their strategy, and their unconscious super-intention.
2. **Identify the Value at Stake:** Note the opening and closing emotional charge.
3. **Map the Action/Reaction Gerunds:**
   - *Beat 1:* Character A [Intimidating through cold silence] → Character B [Deflecting with flippant humor].
   - *Beat 2:* Character A [Cornering with physical evidence] → Character B [Feigning wounded innocence].
   - *Beat 3 (Turning Point):* Character A [Delivering the fatal ultimatum] → Character B [Collapsing into tears and confessing].
4. **Contrast Verbal Rhythms:** Ensure characters contrast in sentence length, pace, and vocabulary.

## 9. Dialogue Diagnostic Checklist

1. What is the active behavioral action (gerund) underneath this line?
2. Does the scene pass the **Cover-the-Names Test**?
3. Does any line contain characters explaining facts both parties already know?
4. Is any line written **on-the-nose**, stating raw subtext directly?
5. Can flat two-character arguments be restructured into a dynamic **Trialogue**?
6. Are any tactical beats being repeated without escalating?
7. Is the operative, punchy word anchored at the very end of the sentence?
8. Are crutches removed (parentheticals, ellipses, exclamation marks, *"Look"*, *"Listen"*)?
9. Does the dialogue challenge rather than agree?
10. Can any line of dialogue be replaced with an active, silent physical gesture?
11. Are long speeches broken up by behavioral interruptions or reactions?
12. Is the dialogue free of first-choice clichés?
