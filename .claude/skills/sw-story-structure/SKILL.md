# Story Structure (sw-story-structure)

> **Output Language:** English (or matching user query language). All terminology anchors to original craft terms (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)).

This skill merges six premier structural frameworks: **Syd Field** (*Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting* — paradigm, plot points), **Blake Snyder** (*Save the Cat!* — BS2 15 beats, 40-card board), **Robert McKee** (*Story* — structural hierarchy, story triangle, inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis-climax-resolution), **Julian Hoxter** (*Storytelling for Film and Television* — 9 beats, A/B stories), **Neill Hicks** (*Writing the Active Screenplay* — three acts as Attraction/Anticipation/Satisfaction, ten essential elements), and **Classic Dramatic Theory** (*Lu Jun, Gu Zhongyi* — four-stage progression, opening techniques, climax integrity, transition mechanics, decisive endings, dynamic variation).

They are fully complementary:
- **Field** provides the anatomical skeleton.
- **Snyder** provides precise page-number pacing.
- **McKee** provides the underlying psychological principles and value shifts.
- **Hoxter** provides concrete beat nomenclature.
- **Hicks** provides audience psychology and suspense mechanics.
- **Dramatic Craft** provides operational techniques for compression, reversals, and scenic momentum.

## 1. Structural Hierarchy (Robert McKee)

- **Structure:** A strategic selection of events from the characters' life stories composed into a strategic sequence to arouse specific emotions and express a specific view of life.
- **Event:** Meaningful change expressed through a **Value** (life/death, love/hate, freedom/slavery, truth/lie—binary charges that flip positive or negative). A rainstorm in East Africa is a coincidence; a rainmaker coaxing rain from conflict is a story event.
- **Hierarchy:**
  1. **Beat:** An exchange of action/reaction. The smallest unit of dramatic behavior.
  2. **Scene:** An action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one value with a degree of perceptible significance (minor change).
  3. **Sequence:** A series of 2 to 5 scenes that culminates with greater impact than any previous scene (moderate change). Every sequence should have a clear title/objective.
  4. **Act:** A series of sequences that peaks in a major reversal scene of climax (major change).
  5. **Story:** A series of acts culminating in the ultimate Story Climax—resulting in an **absolute and irreversible** change of value (scene/sequence/act changes can be reversed; story climaxes cannot).
- **No Scene Without a Turn:** Mark the value polarity (+/-) at the start of every scene and at the end. If the charge doesn't change, ask: *"Why does this scene exist?"* If it only delivers exposition, cut it and weave the information into an active scene.
- **Plot:** Navigating through hazardous terrain. All stories possess plot.

### The Story Triangle (Defining Structural Mode)
- **Top Vertex — Archplot (Classical Design):** Active protagonist, external conflict, continuous time, linear causality, closed ending. The mirror of the human mind; commands the largest audience.
- **Bottom Left — Miniplot (Minimalism):** Open ending, internal conflict, passive protagonist, multiple protagonists (*The Accidental Tourist*, *Parenthood*, *Pelle the Conqueror*).
- **Bottom Right — Antiplot (Anti-structure):** Non-linear time, coincidence, inconsistent reality (*After Hours*, *Weekend*, *Who Framed Roger Rabbit*).
- **Below the Baseline — Nonplot (Static):** Stasis, slice-of-life vignettes without value change.
- **The Screenwriter's Imperative:** Master the classical archplot before experimenting with subversions (Bergman, Fellini, Godard, and Altman all mastered classical structure first). Never be different merely for the sake of being different.

### Three Structural Forms (Gu Zhongyi / Lawson)
1. **Open Form (Chronological / Panoramic):** Presents the entire plot on screen from beginning to end (*Romeo and Juliet*, *The Merchant of Venice*). Expansive, vibrant, shows full character development over time; risk: wide breadth can dilute psychological depth.
2. **Closed Form (Retrospective / Analytical):** Begins near the climax; back-story is unpacked incrementally through present-tense conflict (*A Doll's House*, *Ghosts*). Highly concentrated, relentless momentum, deep psychological excavation; risk: can feel claustrophobic or overly dialogue-heavy if current action stalls. Rule: Retrospective material must always serve current conflict.
3. **Tableau Form (Ensemble / Mosaic):** Large cast, minimal linear plot (*The Lower Depths*, *Sunrise*, *The Teahouse*). Slow external progression, intense subterranean tension, held together by a unifying social/thematic canvas rather than a single protagonist's drive.

## 2. The Three-Act Paradigm & Plot Points (Syd Field)

```
       Act I: Setup             Act II: Confrontation           Act III: Resolution
        Pages 1–30                   Pages 30–90                    Pages 90–120
                       [Plot Point I]                [Plot Point II]
                        (Pages 25–27)                 (Pages 85–90)
```
- **Rule of Thumb:** 1 script page = 1 screen minute.
- **Plot Point:** An incident, episode, or event that hooks into the action and spins it around in a different direction. A script has many minor turns, but Plot Points I and II are the load-bearing anchors.
- **Four Cornerstones Known Before Writing:**
  1. The Ending (Resolution).
  2. The Beginning (Setup).
  3. Plot Point I.
  4. Plot Point II.
  *You don't need every fine detail, but you must know what happens at the end before you type scene one.*
- **Fulcrum Action Method:** Anchor the central midpoint action first. Work backward to deduce the lowest possible starting point, and forward to deduce the inevitable societal/personal fallout at the climax.
- **The First 10 Pages Must Establish:**
  1. Who is the main character?
  2. What is the dramatic premise (what is this story about)?
  3. What is the dramatic situation (the circumstances surrounding the action)?
- **Bookends:** Beginnings and endings echo each other (begins on a road, ends on a road; begins with a boxing match, ends with a boxing match).
- **The Sequence:** A series of scenes connected by a single overarching idea (e.g., the Wedding, the Chase, the Bank Heist). Determine the sequence idea first (the cup), then pour in the specific actions (the coffee).

## 3. Blake Snyder’s BS2 (Save the Cat!) 15 Beats

Standard 110-page feature grid:

| # | Beat Name | Page | Core Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Opening Image** | 1 | Tone, genre, scale. A "before" snapshot of the hero's flawed world. Mirror opposite of the Final Image. |
| 2 | **Theme Stated** | 5 | Usually spoken by another character to the hero. What the movie is really about; the core thematic debate. |
| 3 | **Set-Up** | 1–10 | Introduce all main players; establish the "Six Inherent Flaws" that must be fixed; ticking clock / impending storm. |
| 4 | **Catalyst** | 12 | The telegram, the firing, catching a lover cheating, 3 days to live. Good news in disguise or bad news knocking. |
| 5 | **Debate** | 12–25 | The last chance to say "This is crazy." Protagonist hesitates, resists, or seeks alternatives. Poses a core question. |
| 6 | **Break into Two** | 25 | Cannot occur later than page 25. The protagonist **makes an active choice** to step into Act II. No accidental drifts. |
| 7 | **B Story** | 30 | The relationship / love / mentor story that carries the spiritual theme. B-story characters mirror Act I counterparts. |
| 8 | **Fun and Games** | 30–55 | **The Promise of the Premise.** The trailer moments, poster scenes, and core genre set pieces. |
| 9 | **Midpoint** | 55 | False Victory or False Defeat. Stakes are raised; ticking clock tightens. Direct structural opposite of All Is Lost. |
| 10 | **Bad Guys Close In** | 55–75 | External opposition regroups and escalates; internal cracks fracture the hero's team/alliances. |
| 11 | **All Is Lost** | 75 | The false opposite of the Midpoint. Worse than the beginning. **Whiff of Death** (a mentor dies, symbolic collapse). |
| 12 | **Dark Night of the Soul** | 75–85 | 5 seconds to 5 minutes. The hero is broken and must shed the old ego/flaw to absorb the theme. |
| 13 | **Break into Three** | 85 | The epiphany. Driven by B-story revelation; A-story and B-story fuse into an actionable, transformed battle plan. |
| 14 | **Finale** | 85–110 | Executing the transformed plan. Villains dispatched from low to high; hero alters the world; synthesis achieved. |
| 15 | **Final Image** | 110 | The visual proof that authentic change has taken place. The direct contrast to the Opening Image. |

### The 40-Card Index Board
- **Four Horizontal Rows of 10 Cards Each:**
  - Row 1: Pages 1–25 (Act I to Break into Two).
  - Row 2: Pages 25–55 (Act II-A to Midpoint).
  - Row 3: Pages 55–85 (Act II-B to Break into Three).
  - Row 4: Pages 85–110 (Act III Finale to Final Image).
- **Every Card Must Display:**
  - **Emotional Polarity Shift (`+/-`):** How does the emotional charge flip from scene start to scene end?
  - **Conflict Vectors (`><`):** Who has the objective? Who stands in their way? Who wins the beat? (One conflict per scene).

## 4. Julian Hoxter’s Nine-Beat Model & Linear Pacing

- **Act I (<30 mins):** **Basic Exposition** (Hero's flaw/desire + Inciting Incident) → **Debate** (Desire vs. obstacle; first commitment is weak/naïve).
- **Act II (~45–60 mins in two halves):** **Initial Progress** → **Raising the Stakes** (Complications multiply) → **Midpoint** (Second commitment; no turning back; protagonist becomes proactive) → **The Push** (Gaining ground) → **Crisis** (Lowest point + major revelation; emotional vulnerability).
- **Act III (<30 mins):** **Confrontation** (Execution of final plan meeting counter-assault) → **Resolution** (Goal achieved, theme proved, world changed; lingering emotional reverberation).
- **A-Story vs. B-Story:**
  - **A-Story:** The visible outer goal, tangible plot engine.
  - **B-Story:** The vital emotional relationship where true vulnerability happens. In Act II-B, the B-story provides the emotional ammunition needed to solve Act III.
- **William Goldman’s Ten Commandments of Screenwriting:**
  1. Thou shalt not rob the protagonist of their own crisis.
  2. Thou shalt not make life easy for the protagonist.
  3. Thou shalt not preach or argue for the sake of arguing.
  4. Thou shalt not rely on cheap shock tactics or unearned mystification.
  5. Thou shalt respect the audience's intelligence.
  6. Thou shalt know thy fictional world as God knows His creation.
  7. When elements are rich, do not add artificial complexity.
  8. Drive the character relentlessly into their deepest conflict.
  9. Avoid on-the-nose dialogue; every line must carry subtext.
  10. Rewrite, refine, and polish relentlessly.

## 5. Neill Hicks: Attraction, Anticipation, Satisfaction

- **Act I — Attraction:** Hook the audience and make them genuinely invest in walking beside the protagonist.
- **Act II — Anticipation:** Manage suspense; keep the audience guessing the next move and guessing wrong.
- **Act III — Satisfaction:** Deliver the promised climax, resolving all setups in an unexpected, emotionally rewarding manner.
- **The Ten Essential Elements Checklist:**
  1. Ruthless pruning of backstory (retain only what directly fuels the goal).
  2. Prohibit arbitrary flashbacks.
  3. Clear inner psychological need.
  4. Urgent inciting stimulus.
  5. Tangible external goal.
  6. Preparation / acquisition of skills.
  7. Adversary force must be perceptibly stronger than the hero.
  8. Self-revelation / epiphany.
  9. Deep-seated obsession / fatal flaw.
  10. The protagonist must earn the victory with their own hands.
- **The Five Premise Questions:** Who? Wants what? Why now? Who opposes? What is the outcome?

## 6. Classic Dramatic Craft & Scenic Momentum (Lu Jun / Chinese Dramaturgy)

### Fast Dramatic Entry (In Medias Res)
- Cut close to the crisis point: avoid prolonged domestic prologues before conflict ignites.
- Single, sharp dramatic focus: avoid spreading opening energy across disconnected vignettes.
- Action over explanation: launch with active friction, not verbal backstory recaps.

### The Clear Dramatic Line: Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication
- **Subtraction:** Cut superfluous characters and secondary events that dilute the central thematic spine.
- **Addition:** Provide concrete behavioral and emotional justification for seemingly irrational character decisions.
- **Multiplication:** Take the core conflict or comedic/dramatic premise and heighten its stakes exponentially.

### Climax Integrity: Preventing Structural Climax Diseases
1. **The Absent Climax:** Diffuse, equal-weight scenes resembling a static slideshow with no crowning turning point.
2. **The Premature Climax:** Blowing the highest stakes early in the first act with nowhere left to escalate.
3. **The Slippery Climax:** Loud screaming or fast action that spins its wheels without actually shifting character values or relationships.
4. **The Artificial Climax:** Forcing unearned melodrama (sudden slaps, instant suicides, artificial divorces) without character necessity.

### Smooth Transitions & Crisis Resolution
- Never resolve a crisis via top-down administrative intervention or sudden coincidental rescues.
- The turnaround must stem from the protagonist’s active, decisive choice—preferably a single, concrete, ingenious tactical action that demonstrates their moral evolution.

### The Decisive Ending
- **The Resonant Crack:** Climax and resolution coincide in a sharp, definitive image.
- **The Open Question:** Leaves the ethical dilemma lingering directly in the audience’s lap.
- **The Echo:** Bookends the opening image with an altered, poignant counterpart.

## 7. Inciting Incident & Progressive Complications (McKee)

### The Inciting Incident
- Radically unbalances the protagonist's life, tilting it heavily positive or negative.
- The protagonist must react (even active refusal is a reaction).
- Launches the conscious quest to restore balance; in complex heroes, it simultaneously awakens an unconscious counter-desire.
- **Must occur on screen** and pose the central dramatic question (the obligatory scene the audience now anticipates).
- Timing: Usually within the first 10–15% of runtime.

### Progressive Complications
- The character faces progressively stronger resistance and crosses **Points of No Return**: every widening gap eliminates lower-effort actions.
- **The Law of Conflict:** Nothing moves forward without conflict. Conflict cannot be paused or coasted through without dramatic energy collapsing.
- **The Gap:** The protagonist takes the smallest conservative action to achieve their desire → the world reacts more forcefully or unexpectedly than anticipated → the protagonist is forced into a higher-risk second action.
- **Escalation Hierarchy:** Every defeat must arm the protagonist with one new crucial realization. If two consecutive failures yield identical information, merge them into one.

### Act Climax Design
- Act I Climax (~25%): Definite commitment; crossing the boundary into the new world.
- Midpoint Climax (~50%): False peak or false valley; clock starts ticking.
- Act II Climax (~75–80%): All Is Lost / Dark Night of the Soul; old strategies die.
- Act III Climax (~90–95%): Irreversible resolution of the core dramatic question.
- **Rhythm Rule:** The climaxes of the final two acts cannot share the same emotional charge (never stack unearned euphoria on top of euphoria; contrast sharpens emotion).

## 8. Crisis, Climax, and Resolution (McKee)

- **Crisis:** The ultimate decision. The protagonist has exhausted every conventional tactic and is confronted with a genuine dilemma: choosing between two irreconcilable goods or the lesser of two evils. Must be a deliberate, focused, static beat—never rushed or pushed off-screen.
- **Climax:** The crowning reversal of the story. Does not require physical explosions or screaming, but must be saturated with profound meaning. Aristotle: *"Inevitable and unexpected."*
- **Governing Image:** A single, unforgettable visual metaphor that encapsulates the climax and the controlling idea (e.g., the handcuffs in *Greed*, the stripped apartment in *The Conversation*).
- **Resolution:**
  - Settles any remaining secondary/subplot threads not resolved inside the main climax.
  - Showcases the real-world impact and ripple effects of the climax.
  - Gives the audience a deliberate breath to absorb the emotional resonance before the credits roll.

## 9. Non-linear, Multi-Strand, and Ensemble Structures

- **Non-Chronological:** Flashback structures (*Reservoir Dogs*), fractured memory (*(500) Days of Summer*), cyclical structures (*Pulp Fiction*). Every individual sequence must still obey internal dramatic progression.
- **Multi-Strand & Ensemble:** Interweaving multiple character journeys (*Traffic*, *Magnolia*, *Babel*). Linked together by **Reverberation** (variations on a shared central theme) rather than mechanical causality.
- **Flashback Discipline:** Never use a flashback to fix boring exposition. The audience must feel an urgent, burning need to know *before* the past is revealed. Prefer present-tense action that forces backstory to surface organically.

## 10. Comprehensive Structural Diagnostic Checklist

1. **Logline Clarity:** Can the story be distilled into one active sentence (Protagonist + Inciting Incident + Conflict + Stakes)?
2. **Anchor Points:** Are the Ending, Beginning, Plot Point I, and Plot Point II clearly identified? Is the Midpoint a true False Victory or False Defeat?
3. **Pacing Discipline:** Does the Catalyst hit around page 12? Does the Break into Two happen before page 25?
4. **Active Hero:** Does the protagonist make their own sovereign decision to cross into Act II, rather than being dragged passively?
5. **Dynamic Value Polarity:** Does every scene turn from positive to negative (+ to -) or negative to positive (- to +)? If a scene stays flat, is it merely delivering idle exposition?
6. **Point of No Return:** Does each obstacle in Act II raise the stakes and eliminate easier options? Are there repetitive, low-stakes scenes ("sagging middle")?
7. **Adversary Supremacy:** Is the opposing force significantly more powerful and resourceful than the protagonist at the outset?
8. **Whiff of Death:** Does Act II-B contain an authentic All Is Lost moment where the hero's old worldview is shattered?
9. **Subplot Integration:** Does the B-Story actively challenge, mirror, or arm the A-Story?
10. **The Climax Dilemma:** Is the Crisis a genuine dilemma between two impossible choices? Does the hero solve the final battle through active moral transformation?

## 11. End-to-End Workflow: From Premise to Master Screenplay

1. **One-Sentence Core:** Craft the premise with ironclad irony, emotional stakes, and audience appeal.
2. **Story Triangle Positioning:** Determine Archplot, Miniplot, or Antiplot. Identify key genre conventions and clichés to subvert.
3. **Five Premise Questions:** Lock down Who, Wants What, Why Now, Who Opposes, and What is at Stake.
4. **Anchor the Ending First:** Establish the final destination and the Governing Image of the climax.
5. **The 15-Beat One-Pager (BS2):** Plot out the 15 beats with target page numbers.
6. **The 40-Card Index Board:** Map each sequence across four rows of 10 scenes, labeling emotional polarity (`+/-`) and conflict dynamics (`><`).
7. **Step Outline:** Write 1–2 sentences per scene describing immediate character intent, obstacle, and value turn.
8. **Pitch Test:** Pitch the 10-minute story orally to gauge genuine emotional engagement.
9. **Detailed Treatment:** Draft a prose treatment in the present tense detailing sensory actions and subtext without formal dialogue.
10. **First Draft (Pages):** Write forward with momentum without self-censoring; prioritize scene conflict and behavioral reality.
11. **Subtractive Compression:** Tighten dialogue, trim late entrances and early exits, eliminate redundant scenes.
12. **Targeted Polish:** Execute passes for distinct character voice, subtext enhancement, visual metaphors, and pacing rhythm.
