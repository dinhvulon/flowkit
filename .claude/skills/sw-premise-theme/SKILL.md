# Premise, Theme, and the Dramatic Core (sw-premise-theme)

> **Output Language:** English (or matching user query language). All terminology anchors to original craft terms (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)).

This skill answers four fundamental questions:
1. **What to write:** Subject selection and discipline.
2. **Why write it:** Premise, Controlling Idea, and the Third Rail (inner wound).
3. **From what angle:** Narrative perspective and the Dramatic Core.
4. **How to pitch it in one sentence:** High-concept loglines and pitch hooks.

Synthesized from **Lajos Egri** (*The Art of Dramatic Writing*), **Robert McKee** (*Story*), **Neill Hicks** (*Writing the Active Screenplay*), **Lisa Cron** (*Story Genius*), **Julian Hoxter** (*Storytelling for Film and Television*), **Blake Snyder** (*Save the Cat!*), and **Classic Dramatic Theory** (*Lu Jun, Lu Xun, Wei Minglun, Luo Huaizhen*).

## 1. Premise is a Tyrant (Lajos Egri)

- **Every second must possess a premise.** An inventor who does not know the purpose of their device, or a runner who does not know their destination, is insane; a screenplay full of frantic action without a governing premise is equally unmoored.
- **The Three-Part Premise Equation:**
  - `Character Trait + Conflict (Leads to) + Climax/Resolution`
  - *Example:* "Frugality leads to waste." (Frugality = Character; Leads to = Conflict; Waste = Resolution).
  - *Classic Examples:*
    - *Romeo and Juliet:* Great love defies even death.
    - *King Lear:* Blind trust leads to destruction.
    - *Macbeth:* Ruthless ambition leads to self-destruction.
    - *Othello:* Jealousy destroys itself and the object of its love.
    - *A Doll's House:* Inequality in marriage breeds inevitable disaster.
- **The Author's Conviction:** A premise without personal conviction is hollow. *"You cannot write anything you do not fiercely believe in."* The premise is never stated explicitly in dialogue; the entire dramatic action exists to prove it.
- **One Story, One Premise:** A script cannot pursue two premises simultaneously; two premises pull the narrative in opposite directions. Sub-premises may exist only to enrich and substantiate the primary premise.
- **The Premise Dictates the Inevitable Chain of Events:**
  - *Case Study (A Doll's House):* Needs a married couple → Helmer obsesses over social respectability → Nora's action must threaten his status while being done out of devotion to him → Involves money → Theft would shift the premise into a critique of theft → Emergency medical funds → Helmer would rather die than borrow → Only her father can co-sign → **Her father must be dead or dying** → Nora must forge his signature.
  - *"The premise is a tyrant: it permits only one inevitable path."*
- **The Obligatory Scene as Proof:** The climax must directly validate the premise. If the premise is vague, the climax inevitably falls flat.
- **Character Dialectic:** Thesis (external demand) + Antithesis (character desire) → Synthesis (ultimate resolution).

## 2. The Controlling Idea = Value + Cause (Robert McKee)

- **Premise vs. Controlling Idea:**
  - **Premise:** The speculative spark: *"What would happen if...?"* (e.g., *Jaws*, *Kramer vs. Kramer*). Does not need to stay in the finished work.
  - **Controlling Idea:** A single, definitive declarative sentence:
    - *"Justice prevails because the protagonist is more violent than the criminal"* (*Dirty Harry*).
    - *"Justice prevails because the detective is smarter than the criminal"* (*Columbo*).
    - *"Tyranny flourishes because it is backed by corrupt foreign powers"* (*Missing*).
    - *"Happiness is achieved when one learns to love unconditionally"* (*Groundhog Day*).
- **Aesthetic Commandment:** Storytelling is the creative demonstration of truth through behavior and action—**never through expository lecturing**. Show the truth through consequences.
- **Deduced from the Climax:** Look at your final climax: *What value has been won or lost? What is the primary cause that brought it about?* The climax reveals the true meaning of the work.
- **Idea vs. Counter-Idea (Dialectical Rigor):**
  - Alternate dramatic sequences to give equal firepower to both sides of the argument. If the antagonist is a fool, the protagonist’s victory is hollow.
  - Propaganda treats art like a scalpel; great drama offers clear-eyed confrontation with human nature.
- **Three Categories of Controlling Ideas:**
  1. **Idealistic (Upbeat):** Individual triumph, spiritual ascension, love conquers all.
  2. **Pessimistic (Downbeat):** Inevitable defeat by corruption or flawed human nature (*Chinatown*: Evil triumphs because corruption is woven into the human heart).
  3. **Ironic (Double-Edged):**
     - *Positive Irony (Redemption):* The obsessive pursuit of success or wealth destroys you, but recognizing this in time and letting go grants true spiritual redemption (*Kramer vs. Kramer*, *Out of Africa*).
     - *Negative Irony (Punishment):* Achieving your obsessive ambition costs you your soul and leaves you utterly ruined (*Wall Street*, *Nixon*).
- **Style Born from Friction:** Tone is determined by the mismatch between character nature and event nature (e.g., an absurd, pedantic scholar thrust into a tragic societal purge creates *smiling tragedy / tearful comedy*).

## 3. The Third Rail: Desire vs. Misbelief (Lisa Cron)

- **Story is Not Just Plot:** Plot is what happens on screen. Story is how what happens impacts someone pursuing a high-stakes goal and **forces them to undergo internal psychological transformation**.
- **The Third Rail (The Internal Engine):** The protagonist's desperate conscious yearning versus the internal **Misbelief** holding them back. All plot events, scene obstacles, and sensory details must plug into this third rail.
- **The Nature of the Misbelief:** It is not merely a "flaw" or random wound. The protagonist fiercely believes this worldview is correct because, at a critical moment in their formative past, it saved or protected them (e.g., *"If I never let anyone close, I can never be betrayed"*).
- **The Origin Scene:** The exact moment in the protagonist's history where the misbelief took root. Must be vividly conceived with four beats:
  1. What was the old, innocent belief?
  2. What shocking event shattered it?
  3. What immediate goal did they formulate to survive?
  4. What false conclusion did they lock into their identity?
- **Three Internal Turning Points in the Story:** Each progressive stage moves the hero closer to their goal, but the rising stakes force the misbelief to clash against reality, until the misbelief shatters at the Climax/Epiphany.
- **The Climax is an Internal Epiphany:** The true ending is the exact moment the misbelief is defeated and the hero sees reality through a transformed lens.

## 4. Hicks' Five Premise Questions & Ten Essential Elements

- **The Five Premise Questions:**
  1. Who is the protagonist?
  2. Who or what is the primary opposing force?
  3. Why must they fight (what is the incompatible conflict)?
  4. What profound change results from the outcome?
  5. Why is the protagonist compelled to act right now?
- **Inner Need vs. Outer Goal:**
  - **Outer Goal:** The visible, tangible objective the audience can track.
  - **Inner Need:** The moral or emotional quality the hero lacks (compassion, forgiveness, self-worth). The audience stays engaged because the hero *cannot achieve the outer goal without satisfying the inner need*.

## 5. Theme is Constant, Attitudes Evolve (Julian Hoxter)

- **The Sweet Spot:** The theme remains constant throughout the film; what changes is the characters' attitude toward it.
- **The 4-Character Thematic Web:**
  - Anchor the theme (e.g., Loyalty vs. Ambition).
  - Place four key characters around the theme, each embodying a distinct, polarized attitude (the Idealist, the Cynic, the Pragmatist, the Zealot). Their ongoing collisions explore every facet of the moral debate.
- **Obstacle vs. Antagonist:** An avalanche is an obstacle; a rival mountaineer who cuts your rope to survive is an antagonist. Only the antagonist embodies and challenges the story’s theme.

## 6. High-Concept Loglines & Pitch Hooks (Snyder & Hoxter)

- **Blake Snyder’s 4 Components of a Great Logline:**
  1. **Irony:** The conceptual hook that catches the imagination (*"A cop visits his estranged wife in LA, only for her office tower to be seized by terrorists"*).
  2. **Compelling Mental Picture:** Immediately triggers a cascade of cinematic scenes in the listener’s mind.
  3. **Clear Audience & Cost:** Indicates tone, genre, and production scale.
  4. **Killer Title:** Clever, evocative, and tonally aligned (*Legally Blonde*, *Liar Liar*).
- **Primal Stakes:** Ground the stakes in fundamental human survival drives: life and death, hunger, sexual desire, protecting offspring, fear of mortality, loss of freedom.
- **The Hoxter Logline Formula:**
  - `[Inciting Incident] + [Flawed Protagonist with Vivid Descriptor] + [Must Overcome Monumental Obstacle/Antagonist] + [To Achieve Vital Goal] + [Before Disaster Strikes (Stakes)]`.

## 7. Subject Selection & Thematic Pruning (Lu Jun / Lu Xun)

- **Lu Xun’s Dictum:** *"Be rigorous in selecting subject matter, and excavate deeply. Never puff up petty, meaningless anecdotes into a narrative."*
- **The Four Errors of Subject Selection:**
  1. **Chasing Grandiosity:** Equating vast historical events with artistic depth, or confusing a noble protagonist with a compelling drama.
  2. **Trend-Chasing:** Jumping onto current industry fads where a thousand scripts share identical formulas.
  3. **Lack of Inherent Conflict:** Mistaking a philosophical topic or static situation for dramatic action.
  4. **Mismatched Scale:** Cramming the scope of an epic historical multi-part saga into a single tight feature without focusing the core.
- **The Golden Rule: Small, Pure, with a Strong Dramatic Core:**
  - Choose a focused, tangible inciting incident.
  - A story whose core spine cannot be clearly articulated in five minutes is structurally flawed.
  - Make simple conflicts profoundly complex by digging into psychological and societal repercussions.

## 8. Excavating Thematic Depth (Liyi)

- **Goethe’s Law:** *"The highest mark of originality lies in taking a familiar subject and developing it to its ultimate expressive depth."*
- **Four Techniques of Thematic Excavation:**
  1. **Big World, Small Story:** Place an intimate personal crisis against the backdrop of massive historical or societal upheaval (personal honor colliding with systemic shifts).
  2. **Psychological Excavation:** Unpack the contradictory impulses of real human beings under duress.
  3. **Designing the Ending:** Excavating theme is an ongoing dialogue with the ending. How does the final beat recontextualize everything that came before?
  4. **The Power of the Symbol:** Anchor the thematic debate into a central sensory object or visual motif.
- **Historical Authenticity:** The truth of historical drama does not lie in pedantic costume or architecture recreation; it lies in the authentic recreation of human psychology, emotional pressures, and existential stakes.

---

## 9. Fresh Perspective & Point of View

- Familiar genres become electric when viewed through an unexpected lens:
  - Frame the conflict through the observant bystander or the one who has the most to lose.
  - **Indirect Framing:** Like the classic painter who renders a "temple deep in the mountains" not by showing walls, but by painting a lone monk drawing water from a ravine. Find the unexpected vantage point that reveals the whole truth.
  - **Defamiliarization:** Take a mundane social dynamic and place it under an intense, surreal, or high-stakes metaphorical microscope.

## 10. The Dramatic Core (Xi He)

- **Tolstoy:** *"The most essential thing in any work of art is that it possesses a central focus... a point of magnetic convergence that cannot be fully exhausted in words alone."*
- **The Dramatic Core:** The indispensable creative device or dynamic engine that gives the story its unique heartbeat. Without it, the story collapses into generic convention.
- **Four Manifestations of the Dramatic Core:**
  1. **A Singular Decisive Action:** An audacious, shocking choice that redraws all social relationships.
  2. **A Loaded Dramatic Object:** A physical prop loaded with immense emotional, legal, or moral weight.
  3. **An Impossible Plight:** An ethical or situational trap that leaves no painless exit.
  4. **A Resonant Catchphrase or Song:** A recurring motif whose meaning radically reverses across the acts.

---

## 11. Authentic Conflict vs. False Drama

- **Three Tests of True Conflict:**
  1. **Rooted in Reality:** Genuine stakes, not manufactured misunderstandings that a single 30-second conversation would solve.
  2. **Significant:** Tangible consequences for life, liberty, or emotional survival.
  3. **Dramatic, Not Debating:** Clashing through behavior, tactics, and action—not academic verbal sparring.
- **Belinsky’s Principle:** True dramatic dialogue occurs when two opposing forces each fight to gain the upper hand, strike at the other's vulnerability, and in doing so, force a brand new relationship into existence.

## 12. End-to-End Workflow: From Spark to Locked Premise

1. **Capture the Spark:** Record the core image or incident that haunts you. Ask: *Why do I care? What makes this story uniquely mine to tell?*
2. **Subject Audit:** Is the premise focused and dramatizable? Are the stakes primal?
3. **Draft the One-Sentence Core:** Character trait + conflict + resolution (Egri equation).
4. **Identify the Controlling Idea:** Value won or lost + primary causal reason (McKee). Determine whether the tone is Idealistic, Pessimistic, or Ironic.
5. **Construct the Third Rail:** Identify the hero's tangible outer desire, the hidden inner wound/need, and the formative Misbelief (Cron). Draft the origin scene.
6. **Answer the Five Premise Questions:** Lock down Who, Opponent, Incompatible Conflict, Resulting Change, and Urgency (Hicks).
7. **Map the Thematic Web:** Distribute distinct attitudes toward the central theme across four key characters (Hoxter).
8. **Isolate the Dramatic Core:** Identify the singular high-stakes device, action, or loaded object that anchors the narrative.
9. **Lock the Logline:** Combine irony, vivid visual imagery, character descriptors, obstacle, and stakes into an irresistible pitch sentence.
10. **The Rigorous Self-Audit:**
    - Is there only one central premise?
    - Does the ending stem from the protagonist's active choice rather than coincidence?
    - Does the theme remain constant while the characters' attitudes evolve?
